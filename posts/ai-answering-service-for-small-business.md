That is the call an AI answering service for small business is supposed to catch. Not the 10am "are you open Saturday" call. The one that arrives while your hands are full.

**The only test that matters.**
Have someone call your line after 9pm and ask a question your script does not cover. What happens next — a clean handoff, a guess, or a polite dead end — is the product. Everything else is packaging.

## The short answer

An AI answering service is enough for a small business when the job is **answer the phone, ask your questions, and get the lead to you**. A voice AI can do that on the first ring, at any hour, from information you uploaded about the business. It will not get tired and it will not "get back to you tomorrow."

It is not enough when the call has to **end with a calendar slot, a card on file, or a job already typed into field-service software**. Some vendors sell those extras. Many do not. Mixing the two in your head is how you buy the wrong thing.

**What it costs, as of 31 August 2026:** live receptionist plans from Ruby start at **$250 a month** for 50 minutes. Smith.ai's live Starter is **$300 a month** for 30 calls. Smith.ai's AI Receptionist Pro is **$150 a month** for 75 calls at $2.00 a call, with a free tier at 25 calls. Flat AI phone plans also sit in a roughly **$20–$200** band. Always model a busy month, not an average one.

**What to judge it on:** the 30 seconds after the caller says something you never wrote down. If the system invents an answer, keep walking.

## What people mean by "AI answering service"

Searchers use three labels for the same shopping trip: AI answering service, AI receptionist, virtual receptionist. They are not the same product.

**A traditional answering service** is a call centre. An operator reads your script, takes a message, and emails or texts you. Fine at volume, weak at knowing your trade.

**A virtual receptionist** is the same idea further upmarket: a smaller team, a longer script, sometimes the authority to book and take payment. You are paying for a person. After-hours is often a different, cheaper team than the one that answered your demo call at 2pm. Ask who is on the line at 11pm.

**An AI answering service** is a voice AI on a real phone line. It holds a conversation, answers from the information you gave it, takes the job details, and passes them on. Some products stop there. Some add calendar booking, CRM updates, or a live agent sitting behind the AI.

The category write-up lives in [AI receptionist for small business](/blog/ai-receptionist-for-small-business). This page is the buying decision: when that tool covers the call, and when you are still shopping for a person.

Callers do not care which label you bought. They care that somebody picked up, sounded like your business, and did not make them press 1.

## What has to happen on the call

For a solo operator — plumber, HVAC tech, electrician, cleaner, landscaper, handyman — a useful call does four things and then gets out of the way.

:::steps
Pick up :: First ring, any hour, including the Saturday the van is empty. A menu is not an answer.
Sound like you :: Service area, hours, what you actually do, what you do not do. That has to come from your information, not a generic web summary.
Qualify :: Name, number, address, what is broken, whether it is leaking / no-cool / no-heat / urgent. Four answers beat a voicemail that says "please call me back."
Hand it over :: A text to you with the details, a recording, a transcript. Something you can act on from the driveway.
:::

If a product does those four, it is doing the job most owners are paying for. Booking the slot, running a card, and opening Jobber or Housecall Pro while the caller is still talking are a fifth, sixth and seventh job. Price them separately. A lot of "AI answering" sales pages blur that line.

Two failure modes show up on real lines.

**The guess.** The caller asks something that is not in the script — a HOA rule, a part you do not stock, a complaint about last Tuesday's tech. An AI that fills the silence with a confident wrong answer costs you more than voicemail. The right behaviour is to stop and hand off to a person.

**The after-hours bait.** The demo is a sharp operator at 2pm. Nights and Sundays go to a different queue, or to a thinner AI prompt, or to voicemail with a cheerful "we'll return your call next business day." For home service, next business day is the competitor who picked up.

## When an AI answering service is enough

It is enough when you are the front desk. One van, one cell, jobs that die if the phone rings four times. The caller needs a human sentence, a few qualifying questions, and a promise that you will call back. They need it now, not at 8am.

That describes most of the owners this category is built for. A plumbing emergency is the sharp version of the same problem; the trade-specific version is in [answering service for plumbers](/blog/answering-service-for-plumbers). HVAC, electrical, cleaning, lawn, pest, garage doors — same shape. The expensive call arrives while you cannot pick up.

AI is a fit when all of these are true:

**Your script is knowable.** Hours, service area, what you fix, what you do not, trip fee, after-hours rate, whether you need the water shut off. If you can write it on one page, a voice AI can ask it.

**The output you need is a lead, not a booked job.** A text with the address and the problem is enough to decide whether you roll tonight.

**After-hours is the point.** You are not buying a nicer 10am receptionist. You are buying the 11pm call. AI does not swap in a junior night team.

**You will live with a handoff on the weird ones.** Complaints, "your tech was just here," anything legal or medical, anything you never documented. Those should go to you, not to a made-up policy.

If that is the job, paying $300–$800 a month for a live operator is a choice, not a requirement. You may still prefer a person. You should know you are paying for judgement and for extras, not for the pickup itself.

## When you still want a person on the line

Buy a live receptionist — or an AI product with a real live-agent backup you have tested — when the call has to finish the transaction.

**The calendar is the product.** Dentists, salons, clinics, lawyers who want the consult on the books before hang-up. Smith.ai charges $1.50 a call to book on the live side and lists Calendly-style scheduling on the AI side, as of 31 August 2026. Ruby lists scheduling on every live plan. If that step is the reason you are shopping, start there, and watch a real booking happen on a recorded call.

**You need a deposit before you roll.** Some live services will take a card. Ruby lists payment collection. Smith.ai live lists it at $1.00 a call. Most low-cost AI phone products will not. Do not assume.

**Someone has to type into the software you already run.** Jobber, Housecall Pro, ServiceTitan, Clio, the practice management tool. Live services and some higher-end AI tools advertise CRM and software hooks. A lot of AI answering stops at a text to the owner. If the handoff is "now re-key this into Jobber," you have bought a message, not a workflow.

**The call is a negotiation.** An angry customer, a billing dispute, a "your tech scratched the floor" call. Judgement is the thing AI is worst at, and the thing a trained operator is paid for.

**You need the same named person every time.** Some virtual receptionist plans sell that. AI will not. If the relationship *is* the receptionist, you are in a different category. See [virtual receptionist pricing](/blog/virtual-receptionist-pricing).

A useful split: **AI for coverage, live for completion.** Coverage is "nobody who wanted to hire you got voicemail." Completion is "the appointment exists, the card is run, the job is in the system." Plenty of owners only need coverage. The expensive mistake is buying coverage and expecting completion.

## What to test before you pay

Skip the feature list. Run these six on a trial or a recorded demo.

1. **Call it at 11pm on a Sunday.** Same voice, same questions, same escalation as Tuesday at 10am? If not, you just met the real product.
2. **Ask something that is not in the script.** "Do you work on that old boiler in the crawlspace at the rental on 4th?" The only acceptable next move is a handoff or a precise "I don't know — I'll have the owner call you." A made-up yes is a one-star review.
3. **Time the first sentence.** If the caller hears a phone tree, a 12-second pause, or "please hold for the next available receptionist," a chunk of after-hours callers are already gone.
4. **See what lands on your phone.** A name and number is a message. Address, problem, urgency, callback window, recording, transcript — that is a lead. Ask where it goes if you are on a roof.
5. **Ask how spam, hang-ups and wrong numbers are billed.** Ruby bills minutes. Smith.ai live says spam is free when Caller ID passes. Smith.ai AI counts answered calls, with a 10% spam allowance and a pre-filter for known spammers. These rules change the real monthly number more than the headline rate.
6. **Ask what happens at the minutes or calls cap.** Voicemail? Overage? A frozen AI? Read the sentence, not the "unlimited" on the hero.

If the vendor will not let you call the production line after hours before you sign, you already have the answer.

Keep the existing number if you can. Forwarding is the usual path. The number on the van stays put, calls get answered. Forwarding does not carry texts. Porting does, and it is slower. Business texting on a new or ported number often needs a one-time carrier registration that takes a few business days. Calls and voicemail should work from day one. If a salesperson says texts will fire the minute you swipe a card, they are skipping that step.

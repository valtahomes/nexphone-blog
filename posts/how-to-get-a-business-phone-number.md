It is 8:15 on a Thursday at the credit union. The business-account specialist is on the LLC form and asks for the company phone number. You give her the cell you have had since 2014. She types it in.

That number is already in a group chat with your sister about school pickup. It is on two customer threads, a supplier, and a missed call from a man who wants a water-heater quote. By next week it will also be on the Google listing.

You need a business phone number. This is how to get one, what “free” means, and what the paid options cost.

:::callout
A business number is a number customers can call without walking into your personal life.
Free options exist. They have limits. Paid phone systems start cheap. Live answering services that happen to include a number are a different product at a different price. Know which you are buying.
:::

## The short answer

**How you get one:** choose a provider, pick a local or toll-free number, and either take a new number or port the one already on the van. The number lives in an app on the phone you already carry. You do not need a second physical handset.

**Free:** [Google Voice](https://workspace.google.com/products/voice/) Basic is **$0 a month** as of 17 September 2026. An existing mobile number is required. Your personal cell is also free, and it is a poor business line.

**Paid, for the number itself:** Google Voice Starter is **$10 a month** and Standard is **$20**. [Quo](https://www.quo.com/pricing) starts at **$19 per user per month**. [Grasshopper](https://grasshopper.com/pricing/) advertises from **$14 a month**. All three figures are from those vendors’ public pricing pages on 17 September 2026.

**Paid, if what you want is a person on the line:** that is an answering service, not a phone number. [Ruby](https://www.ruby.com/plans-and-pricing/) lists **$250 a month** for 50 receptionist minutes, same date. The industry breakdown of those bills lives in [what an answering service costs](/blog/answering-service-cost).

**For an LLC:** you can file with a personal cell. You should not leave that cell on the bank account, the EIN, the Google listing and the invoices, because those records last.

:::stats
$0/mo :: Google Voice Basic, 17 September 2026 — free number, existing mobile required
$10–$20/mo :: Google Voice Starter and Standard, same date
$19–$33/user/mo :: Quo Starter and Business, same date
$14/mo :: Grasshopper’s advertised starting price, same date
$250/mo :: Ruby’s 50-minute live receptionist plan — a different product
:::

## Why the number on the van cannot be your cell

A personal cell works until the business does. Then three things happen at once.

Customers text it at 9pm about a quote. Your family texts it about dinner. You cannot tell which buzz is which without looking, and you are often not in a position to look.

You cannot hand the line to anyone. A helper, a spouse covering Friday, a second tech — they do not get “the business phone.” They get your pocket.

You cannot leave. Sell the company, take a week off, or stop answering after 6pm, and the number on Google is still you. Changing it later means the van wrap, the invoices, the Google listing and every regular customer.

A business number does one job: it is the public line. Calls and texts for the company land there. Your cell stays yours.

## Free options

There are two honest free paths.

### 1. Keep using your personal cell

Cost: $0 extra. Setup: nothing.

You already know the failure mode. The number is public, it never stops being public, and every future customer who saves it still has it after you “switch.” Treat this as the default you are leaving, not a plan.

### 2. Google Voice Basic at $0

[Google’s Voice plans page](https://workspace.google.com/products/voice/) on 17 September 2026 lists **Basic at $0 a month**: one number on your devices, unlimited domestic calling and texting, subject to Google’s acceptable use policy. You must already have a mobile number to open it.

What that gets you: a real second number, an app, voicemail that turns into text. For a side project, a consultant who does not want a cell on a website, or an LLC that needs *a* number this afternoon, it is the legitimate free option.

What sits on the paid Voice plans, not Basic: 24/7 support, a promise that the number will not be reclaimed for inactivity, three-way calling, transfers, call recording, desk-phone support, auto attendants. Starter is $10 a month. Standard is $20. Google also shows Standard at $10 for the first six months, then $20. Budget the $20.

If you want the longer version of how Voice behaves as a company line, that is in [Google Voice for business](/blog/google-voice-for-business).

Random “free number” apps exist. They make money on ads, recycled numbers, and numbers that die. If the number is going on an LLC filing or a Google listing, use a company that still publishes a pricing page.

## Paid options, and what they cost

Prices below are from public pages on **17 September 2026**. They move. Check the vendor before you pay.

**Google Voice Starter — $10 a month**
One person. Number is not reclaimed for inactivity. Transfers, recording, desk-phone support, 24/7 Voice support. Still a Google number on your existing devices.

**Google Voice Standard — $20 a month**
Starter plus auto attendants and Gemini notes on calls. Team versions are billed per user and need Google Workspace: Starter **$10 per user per month**, Standard **$20**, Premier **$30**.

**Quo Starter — $19 per user per month** ($15 if you pay a year)
One local or toll-free number per user, calling and messaging to US and Canadian numbers, voicemail transcripts. Extra numbers are **$5 a month** each. Business is **$33 per user per month** and adds summaries, group calling, phone menus, auto recording. Scale is **$47**. Quo’s AI agent (Sona) is listed as included, with a credit pool of 1,000 free automation credits on every plan, which they count as 10 calls.

**Grasshopper — from $14 a month**
A classic virtual-number product: greetings, extensions, a number that rings through to you. The public page on 17 September 2026 leads with **$14 a month** and a 7-day trial. It does not spell out the full tier list on that page, so treat $14 as a floor, not a quote for your setup.

**A carrier second line**
Your wireless company will sell you another number on the same handset (dual SIM or a business add-on). You pay their rate. The number still rings you. Nobody answers it while both your hands are in a panel.

**A live receptionist plan that includes a number**
Ruby, Smith.ai and similar sell answering, not a cheap extra line. Ruby’s virtual-receptionist plans on 17 September 2026 start at **$250 a month for 50 minutes**, then $395 / 100 minutes, $720 / 200, $1,725 / 500. Smith.ai’s human-first receptionist page lists **$300 a month for 30 calls**, then $810 / 90, $2,100 / 300, with overage per call. If that is the bill you are looking at, you are shopping for coverage, not a number. Use [the cost guide](/blog/answering-service-cost) and price a busy month, not the headline.

:::callout
Buy a phone number if the job is “customers should not have my cell.”
Buy an answering service if the job is “someone has to pick up when I cannot.”
A $14–$33 VoIP plan does the first job. It does not do the second unless you add answering on purpose.
:::

## Local number or toll-free

**Local** uses an area code your customers recognise. For a plumber, HVAC tech, cleaner or landscaper, this is usually the right default. People call a number that looks like it belongs on their street.

**Toll-free** (800, 888, 877 and the rest) looks like a company with a bigger footprint. It is useful if you take jobs across several area codes, run ads in more than one city, or want one number that does not scream a suburb. It is not automatically more professional, and it is not automatically better for Google.

Most of the VoIP plans above include a local or a toll-free number in the base price. Extra numbers are a small add-on — Quo lists **$5 a month**. The longer decision sits in [toll-free number for business](/blog/toll-free-number-for-business).

Pick one number and put it everywhere. Two public numbers is how calls get lost.

## A business phone number for an LLC

This is the part people search after the filing receipt shows up.

You do not need a special “LLC phone number” product. You need one stable number that is not your private cell, and you need it on the records that outlive the afternoon you formed the company.

**EIN.** The IRS form asks for a phone number. Whatever you type becomes the number they have.

**Bank.** The credit-union scene at the top of this page. Business accounts want a company phone. If you give them your cell, that cell is now on a bank record.

**Insurance, vendors, 1099s.** Same pattern. The number on the policy is the number they will call in a claim.

**Google Business Profile, website, invoices, van.** This is the customer-facing copy of the same decision. Change it later and you reprint, you update Google, and you hope regulars notice.

A practical order that saves reprinting:

:::steps
Get the number first :: Open the VoIP account or Google Voice and pick the local (or toll-free) number before you finish the bank and the Google listing.
Put it on the EIN and the bank :: One number in both places. Do not invent a second “office line” you will not answer.
Put it on Google, the site and the paperwork :: Same digits on the listing, the invoice footer and the van. Customers should only ever learn one.
Keep your cell off those records :: Your cell can still be the device that rings. It should not be the number the public sees.
:::

If you already filed with your cell, get the business number anyway and update the bank, Google and invoices now. State filings are their own process. Do not assume a phone change on a website updates the secretary of state.

The LLC does not care whether the number is free Google Voice or a $19 VoIP line. The LLC cares that the number still works in three years.

## How to get the number onto the phone you already have

Almost every small operator ends up with two numbers on one handset. That is normal.

A VoIP app (Google Voice, Quo, Grasshopper, and the rest) gives the business line its own keypad, voicemail and — once texting is allowed — its own threads. Your personal cell stays the carrier line. You hear which one is ringing.

A carrier second line does the same thing with a dual SIM or eSIM. You pay the carrier. You still answer it yourself.

You do not need a plastic desk phone. Those exist, and Google Voice and others will talk to one if you want it. For a one-person trades business, the app is the phone.

**New number, forward, or port**

- **New number.** Fast. You pick digits, the app rings, you start putting it on things. Anything already printed still shows the old number.
- **Forwarding.** The number on the van keeps working. Calls follow to the new line. Texts often do not follow, which matters if customers text the number they already have.
- **Porting.** The existing number moves to the new provider for real, calls and (usually) texts included. It takes longer than a new number. Start it before you print a new wrap.

If customers already call you and hang up when you miss it, the follow-up problem is separate from owning the number. That is [missed-call text-back](/blog/missed-call-text-back).

**Texting**

Inbound calls work on day one with every serious provider. Outbound business texting in the US is a carrier rule, not a vendor quirk. Quo’s pricing page on 17 September 2026 lists a Campaign Registry review fee of **$19.50 one-time** and a monthly messaging fee of **$1.50–$3**. Budget a few business days for that registration before you promise customers they can text the new line. Do not plan a launch that depends on texts landing the same afternoon you sign up.

## What to do this afternoon

:::steps
Choose local or toll-free :: Local if your customers are in one area code. Toll-free if you work across several. One public number, not two.
Open the account and pick digits :: Google Voice if the budget is $0–$20 and you are one person. A paid VoIP plan if you need shared access, menus, or a number you will still have after a quiet month.
Get it ringing on your phone :: Install the app, make a test call from a different phone, record the voicemail in your own voice.
Put it on the public surfaces :: Google listing, website, invoices, email signature, van. Then the EIN and the bank if those still show your cell.
Decide what happens when you cannot pick up :: Voicemail is the default on a cheap number. Voicemail is also how after-hours jobs leave.
:::

That last step is the one people skip, and it is the one that costs money. A $10 number that rings a pocket in a crawlspace is still an unanswered phone.

## The short answer

**What Nextiva costs:** as of 24 September 2026, Nextiva publishes **Core at $15 per user per month** billed annually ($23 if you pay month to month), **Engage at $25** annually ($50 monthly), and **Scale at $75** per user per month. XBert, the AI receptionist, is an add-on. Nextiva advertises **AI plans from $99 a month**.

**What you are choosing:** a full communications platform (phone, team chat, video, contact-center tools), a smaller business phone built for a handful of people, a free-ish Google number with sharp limits, or an AI receptionist that sits on a real US business line and handles the call while your hands are full.

**Who should stay:** teams that need omnichannel routing, a proper inbound queue, or one vendor for 15–100 people. Who should look around: a one- to five-person shop whose bill climbed because every extra human is another seat, and whose actual problem is the call that comes in while nobody can pick up.

## What you actually bought

Nextiva is a unified communications product, not an answering service with a phone number taped on. The Core plan, from the [Nextiva pricing page](https://www.nextiva.com/nextiva-pricing) as it stood on 24 September 2026, includes inbound and outbound voice, business SMS, video meetings, call routing, team chat, and a mobile app. Engage adds customer-to-team SMS, a toll-free number and minutes, live chat, and inbound call-center features. Scale is the full customer-experience bundle.

XBert sits next to that stack, not inside the base price. Nextiva’s AI receptionist page lists appointment booking, CRM follow-up, web chat, and after-hours capture. The phone plans mark it as an add-on. The pricing page’s own AI callout starts at **$99 a month**.

That mix is why the invoice surprises people. You approved a business phone. You are paying for a platform, then paying again so the platform will answer.

Nextiva says it is trusted by 100,000 businesses, with 17 years in the market and a 4.6 rating from 8,484 Trustpilot reviews — those figures are Nextiva’s, taken from the same pages on 24 September 2026. It also describes a buyer who is usually larger than a two-truck HVAC company.

## A small-team bill, written out

Do the arithmetic on published rates before you take another demo.

**One person, Core, billed annually, plus the AI starting point**

- Phone: $15
- XBert from $99
- **$114 a month** before taxes and carrier fees

**Three people, Engage, billed annually, plus the same AI starting point**

- Phone: 3 × $25 = $75
- XBert from $99
- **$174 a month**

**The same three people, Engage, billed monthly**

- Phone: 3 × $50 = $150
- XBert from $99
- **$249 a month**

Engage’s monthly rate is double the annual rate on the page Nextiva published on 24 September 2026. If a sales quote used the $25 number and finance is paying the $50 number, that is the gap.

Add a fourth person and the phone line item moves again. That is the design. Per-user pricing is honest for a 25-person office. It is a poor match for a shop where two of the “users” are techs who should never be the front desk.

If the job you are trying to save is a $400 after-hours call-out, a $174–$249 phone bill can still be cheap. The test is whether that bill is the thing catching the call, or a stack you log into after you already missed it.

## Who should keep Nextiva

Stay if most of this is true:

- You already run the company from NextivaONE and switching would mean retraining a real office, not one owner with a van.
- You need inbound queues, skills-based routing, or a contact-center agent seat. Nextiva’s contact-center Essential plan starts at **$75 per agent per month** as of 24 September 2026.
- Video meetings, team chat, and the phone have to live in one product because that is how the staff already works.
- You are heading toward several locations and want one vendor on the way up.
- Someone in the building is paid to administer the phone system.

A franchise, a clinic, a 20-person agency. Nextiva is sized for that. Buying it for a one-van plumbing company is the problem.

## Cheaper phone systems people actually switch to

These are phone systems. They are not ranked. Prices are what each vendor published on 24 September 2026. Taxes, carrier fees, and number porting sit on top.

### Quo

Quo is the current name of the product many people still call OpenPhone. Monthly billing:

- **Starter — $19 per user per month.** One number per user, calling and messaging to US and Canadian numbers, voicemail transcripts. Sona, the AI agent, is included with 1,000 automation credits (Quo counts 1 call as 100 credits, so 10 AI-answered calls).
- **Business — $33 per user per month.** Call summaries and transcripts, phone menus, auto recording, HubSpot and Salesforce, group calling.
- **Scale — $47 per user per month.** AI call tags, inbound phone support, dedicated onboarding.

Annual billing drops those to **$15 / $23 / $35** per user. Extra numbers are **$5 a month**. Extra Sona credit packs run **$25 to $199 a month**, with overage from $1.00 down to $0.45 per AI call depending on the tier.

Three people on Quo Business monthly is **$99**, with 10 AI-answered calls in the base credits. That is the comparison most small teams are doing in their head. If you want a longer look at that product on its own terms, we keep a separate page on [Quo alternatives](/blog/quo-alternatives).

Watch the fair-use policy on “unlimited” US and Canada calling, and budget time for 10DLC registration before outbound texts deliver reliably. Quo publishes a **$19.50** one-time Campaign Registry fee and **$1.50–$3 a month** after that.

### RingCentral

RingCentral is the other full platform in this category. The RingEX phone system is sold in tiers; the public page we pulled on 24 September 2026 did not print a clean per-user dollar figure for RingEX itself, which is typical of that sales motion.

What it did print:

- **AI Receptionist from $39 a month**, described as answering and routing on any phone system
- **Conversation Intelligence from $60**
- **Call Queues Booster at $35**
- **Business SMS Booster at $25**

If Nextiva felt large, RingCentral is the other large option. Use it when you want Microsoft Teams-style calling, a long integration list, and a vendor that will still be on the contract in five years. RingCentral also flags TCR registration fees for SMS. Same carrier rule as everyone else. Nobody in this market texts at full deliverability on day one without that paperwork.

### Google Voice

Google Voice is the number people already have, or think they can get for free. For a real business it is a Workspace add-on with a shared inbox that is thinner than it looks, spam labeling that eats real callers, and a porting story that has trapped more than one owner.

It can work as a second line for a solo operator who will accept those limits. It is a weak nextiva alternative if you left Nextiva because you needed after-hours coverage, a proper menu, or a number you can put on a truck wrap without wondering whether Google will keep it. ## How to leave without killing the number on the van

The number on the truck wrap is the asset, not the Nextiva account.

:::steps
Forward first :: Point the live Nextiva number at the new line for a week. Callers keep dialing the same digits. You find out whether the new setup actually answers before you touch the port.
Port second :: Once you trust the new line, port the number. Calls and texts then live in one place. Forwarding never moves texts, which is why missed-call text-back and two-way SMS need the port, not the forward.
Do not print anything :: Magnets, invoices, Google Business, the website footer — leave them until the losing carrier releases the number. A port that slips a week is cheaper than a reprint.
:::

Ask Nextiva for the account number and a port-ready PIN before you give notice in a way that shuts the account off. You want the losing carrier still alive when the new carrier submits the port.

Outbound business texting on any new US number needs 10DLC registration with the carriers. That is a few business days, sometimes longer. Calls and voicemail should work as soon as the new number is live. Plan the week you switch around that gap, especially if customers currently text the Nextiva number.

## Six questions before you sign the next contract

1. **What is the all-in number for my headcount, on monthly billing, with the AI actually turned on?** Quotes love annual Core rates and a phone-only subtotal.
2. **Who answers at 11pm, and is that person or model included?** An add-on you have to remember to enable is not coverage.
3. **What happens when the AI does not know the answer?** You want a handoff to a human or to you. A confident wrong answer is worse than voicemail.
4. **How do they bill spam, wrong numbers, and hang-ups?** Per-call and per-minute receptionist plans hide money here. Per-user phone plans hide it in extra seats instead.
5. **Can I keep the number on the van, for calls and texts?** If the answer involves a new number “just for now,” you will still be explaining that number at Christmas.
6. **What is the cancellation term?** Month-to-month is common. Annual lock-ins are also common. Read the one in front of you.

If a salesperson cannot walk the 11pm call in one paragraph, you are buying a daytime product.

It is 6:40 on a Thursday evening. A homeowner's water heater has quit and there is a cold puddle spreading across the garage floor. They call the first electrician-plumber combo on the map. Four rings, then a flat robot voice: "The person you are trying to reach is not available."

No business name. No instructions. They hang up and dial the next listing.

That job was lost to a greeting you never recorded. This guide gives you the scripts to fix it today. Pick the one that fits, change the brackets, and record it on your phone in about five minutes.

## The short answer

A good business voicemail greeting runs **15 to 25 seconds** and does four things, in this order:

1. Says your business name, so the caller knows they dialed the right place.
2. Tells them you can't take the call right now.
3. Asks for the specific details you need: name, number, and what the job is.
4. Gives a callback window you can actually hit.

Everything below is that formula adjusted for a situation: after hours, a holiday, a full mailbox, a small team, an emergency.

## Before you record anything

A few rules decide whether a greeting works, and none of them need a studio.

**Record it yourself, in your own voice.** A homeowner calling a local trade wants to hear a person who sounds like they own the van. A stiff, over-read script makes a one-person shop sound like a call center.

**Stand somewhere quiet.** A truck cab with the engine off is fine. A job site with a compressor running is not. Play it back on speaker before you save it.

**Slow down.** Almost everybody records too fast. Say it at the speed you would read a phone number to a stranger.

**Say the business name first.** Callers often forget who they dialed by the time they hear the beep.

**Promise only what you do.** If you say "back within the hour," you need to mean it. A missed promise is worse than a modest one. Say "by end of day" if that is the truth.

## Script 1: The standard everyday greeting

Use this when you are on a job and can't pick up during working hours.

> Hi, you've reached [Business Name]. I'm on a job right now and can't get to the phone. Leave your name, your number, and what you need done, and I'll call you back within [two hours]. If it's urgent, say so at the start of your message. Thanks.

Why it works: it explains the silence (you are working, not ignoring them), it tells the caller exactly what to leave, and it flags urgency so you can triage when you listen.

## Script 2: After-hours greeting

Use this on evenings and weekends when you don't answer at all.

> You've reached [Business Name]. Our office hours are [7 a.m. to 5 p.m., Monday through Friday]. Leave your name, number and address, plus a short description of the problem, and we'll call you back the next business morning. If water is actively leaking or you smell gas, hang up and call [your local emergency number or utility].

Keep that last line if you work in plumbing, gas or electrical. It sends a dangerous situation to the right place and shows callers you take safety seriously.

If you offer emergency service, change the second half:

> For emergencies, press 1 or text this number and say "emergency." We check messages every [30 minutes] until [10 p.m.].

Only write that line if you will do it. Callers who press 1 and hear nothing are angrier than callers who never had the option. For more on running a night line, see our guide to [after-hours answering services](/blog/after-hours-answering-service).

## Script 3: Holiday and closure greeting

Record this ahead of time so you aren't doing it at a barbecue.

> You've reached [Business Name]. We're closed for [Thanksgiving] and will be back on [Monday, November 30th]. Leave your name, number and what you need, and we'll call you back that day. For emergency service during the closure, call [number]. Happy holidays.

Put the **return date** in the recording. Without it, this greeting sits on your line in January and tells customers you are still on vacation.

Set a calendar reminder to swap it back. Owners skip that step. A stale holiday greeting costs you real jobs for weeks before anyone notices.

## Script 4: Mailbox full

When the mailbox fills up, callers hear a recording that says the mailbox can't accept messages and then a dial tone. You cannot fix that with a script, since the carrier plays it, but you can avoid it.

Clear old messages weekly. Most carriers cap the number of saved messages or the total minutes, and the limit is lower than people expect. Check yours in your carrier settings and write it down.

If you are going to be unreachable for a stretch and expect heavy volume, swap in a greeting that moves callers to a different channel:

> You've reached [Business Name]. Our voicemail is filling up fast this week. The quickest way to reach us is to text this number with your name and what you need, or email [address]. We'll get back to you by [end of day].

Texting a business line from a customer's side works at any time. If you plan to text customers first from your business number, that needs a one-time carrier registration that takes a few business days, so set it up before you need it.

## Script 5: Small team greeting

When more than one person shares a line, callers need to know who they will reach and how fast.

> Thanks for calling [Business Name]. We're out on jobs and can't pick up right now. Leave your name, number, address and a short description, and whoever is free first will call you back within [one hour]. If you're calling about an existing job, say the name on the work order.

Two details matter here. "Whoever is free first" tells the customer one person will own the callback, so they don't expect three. "Existing job" separates a new lead from a customer with a question. You can return those two calls in a different order.

## Script 6: Greeting for a business with a text option

If you prefer text to voicemail, say so.

> You've reached [Business Name]. Skip the voicemail if you like and text this number instead. Tell us your name, what you need and your address, and we'll reply faster than we can listen to messages. Or leave a message after the tone.

Many customers would rather text than leave a message. Giving them permission removes friction. Make sure someone watches that inbox, because a text with no reply is worse than a voicemail with no reply.

## What to leave out

**Long thank-yous.** Every second before the beep is a second the caller is deciding whether to wait.

**Your whole service list.** Callers already know what you do. They dialed you.

**Jokes that only land once.** A clever line is fun the first time and grating the fifth time a repeat customer hears it.

**A fax number.** Unless you receive faxes, it only adds seconds.

**Vague callback times.** "As soon as possible" tells the caller nothing. "Within two hours" lets them decide whether to wait or call someone else.

## A quick checklist before you save

- Business name in the first sentence
- Under 25 seconds
- Asks for name, number and job details
- Gives a callback window you will meet
- Includes a safety line if you work in gas, water or electrical
- Holiday version has a return date inside it
- You called your own number and listened to the result

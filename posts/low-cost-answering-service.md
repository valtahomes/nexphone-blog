## The short answer

A **low cost answering service** is usually one of three products wearing the same label: a per-minute live call centre with a small included bucket, a per-call virtual receptionist that starts around **$250–$300 a month**, or a flat-rate AI receptionist that can sit well under that if you stay inside the included minutes.

As of **9 September 2026**, the named live options publish like this. [Ruby](https://www.ruby.com/plans-and-pricing/) starts at **$250 a month for 50 receptionist minutes**. [Smith.ai's live receptionist](https://smith.ai/pricing/receptionists) Starter plan is **$300 a month for 30 calls**, then **$11.50** a call after that. [Smith.ai's AI Receptionist](https://smith.ai/pricing/ai-receptionist) has a free tier at **25 calls**, then **$3.00** a call, and a Pro plan at **$150 a month** for 75 calls.

What you give up as the price drops is minutes, after-hours quality, how the call gets qualified, and whether the bill stays still when volume jumps. The right cheap plan is the one that is still cheap in your busiest month and still useful at 9pm.

## What people mean by "low cost"

Searchers are not looking for a philosophy. They had a missed call, they saw a $1,000 answering-service quote, and they want to know if a $50 plan is a real product or an ad.

It is a real product. It is also three different products, and mixing them up is how you sign the wrong contract.

A live operator reading a script is not the same job as a voice AI that picks up on the first ring. ## The three ways a cheap plan bills you

### Per minute

A base fee, then roughly **$1.00–$2.00 a minute** once you leave the included bucket. Traditional answering services love this shape because it looks small in the ad.

You pay more when calls run long, when callers sit on hold, and when the line attracts junk. Wrong numbers, robocalls and "is this the pizza place?" all become minutes. Rounding up to the next minute is common. A 61-second call billed as two minutes is not a rounding error. It is the business model.

This is the plan that looks cheapest in February and ugliest in June.

### Per call

A bundle of answered calls, then a fee for each one after that. Smith.ai's live plans (checked 9 September 2026) are the clean public example: **30 calls for $300**, **90 for $810**, **300 for $2,100**, with overage from **$11.50** down to **$8.50** a call as the bundle grows.

You pay more when volume climbs. You do not pay extra because a caller talked for four minutes. That is the point of the model, and it is why Smith.ai says they bill per call rather than per minute.

The question to ask is what counts as a call. Hang-ups, voicemail dumps and spam are where per-call plans quietly diverge. Smith.ai's live plans say they do not charge for spam if the forwarded line passes Caller ID. Treat that as a Smith.ai policy, not an industry law. Ask every other vendor the same question in writing.

### Flat monthly

A number that does not move with how many people rang you. This is mostly AI answering, sometimes a very small live bundle with a hard cap.

You pay more when you blow past a minutes cap and overage kicks in, or when "unlimited" turns out to have a fair-use clause. Read that clause. There is always one.

Flat is the only shape that does not punish you for a good month — until the cap. Model the cap, not the adjective.

## What drops off as the price drops

**Under about $100 a month.** You are in AI with a small included bundle, or a per-minute centre with a thin bucket. Expect a cap, overage, or both. Expect to write the questions yourself. Do not expect someone to book the job or take a deposit. After-hours coverage may be the same AI. That is the one place the cheap plan can beat a live desk, because software does not go home. It may also be voicemail with a pretty label. Test it.

**$150 to $400.** Named AI plans with enough calls for a quiet small business live here (Smith.ai AI Pro at $150). Named live plans start here too (Ruby 50 minutes at $250, Smith.ai Starter at $300) and then run out of bundle. This band is where people feel they "upgraded" and still get an overage bill. Count your calls before you sit in it.

**$700 and up.** Live minutes and call bundles that can absorb a normal month without drama. Ruby's 200-minute plan at $720. Smith.ai Basic at $810 for 90 live calls. This is also where scheduling, payments and heavier intake stop being theoretical. You are buying a front desk, not a catcher of leftover rings.

Two extras that are not a price band, and still change the bill.

**After hours.** A live service that is excellent at 10am and outsourced at 11pm is two products. Ruby and Smith.ai both publish 24/7 as included on the plans above. Plenty of cheaper centres do not. If nights are when your expensive jobs come in, read [after hours answering service](/blog/after-hours-answering-service) before you take the daytime demo as proof.

**Contracts.** Annual lock-ins are common on the cheap per-minute end, because that is how they recover the $49 ad. Smith.ai live is month-to-month on the page we checked. Ask. A cheap plan you cannot leave is not cheap.

## How to price it against your own month

Do this on paper before you talk to sales.

:::steps
Count last month's inbound calls :: Not "it feels busy." The actual number, including junk, from your phone bill or call log.
Split day and night :: If half the rings are after 5pm, a plan that gets weak at 5pm is the wrong plan, whatever it costs.
Ask how spam is billed :: Per-minute plans bill it as time. Some per-call plans bill it as a call. A few write it off. Get the rule in email.
Price a busy month, not an average one :: Take your highest month in the last year and run the overage. That is the invoice that matters.
Place a test call at 9pm :: On a Sunday if you can. Hear who answers, what they ask, and how long until your phone buzzes.
:::

If the vendor will not let you test after hours before you sign, you already have the answer.

The four things worth collecting on that test call are the same ones a plumber needs, and they are not a name and a number. Is this urgent. What is the address. Can they describe the job. Is anyone else supposed to approve the spend. An answering service that cannot get those four out of a caller has sold you a voicemail with extra steps.

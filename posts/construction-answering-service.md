A circular saw is running on a roof in July. Your phone is in the truck, under a pile of cut-off shingles. A GC two towns over is awarding a framing package that starts Monday. He will try you once, maybe twice. Then he dials the next sub on the list.

That call is not a message. It is the job.

:::callout
**The only test that matters is who picks up while you cannot.**
Any service can take a name at 10am. Construction lives on the 6:15am GC call and the 7pm homeowner who finally decided. Ask what happens in those two windows, and what a month of that coverage actually costs.
:::

## The short answer

**What it costs:** a live virtual receptionist for a small construction shop usually lands around **$250–$720 a month** on published minute plans, or about **$300 a month** for 30 live calls, with overage after that. Flat AI answering is cheaper per month if you do not need someone to book a calendar or take a card. Prices below are from vendor pages checked on **10 September 2026**. They move.

**What you are choosing:** a call centre that reads a script, a virtual receptionist who can also book and collect payment, or an AI receptionist that answers on the first ring and passes you the lead. Same Google query. Three different jobs.

**What to judge it on:** intake. If the thing that lands on your phone is "John, 555-…, called about a job," you bought a message. If it has the address, the work type, the timeline, and who is calling, you bought a lead you can price from the truck.

## Why construction misses are a different kind of expensive

A plumber who misses an 11pm burst pipe loses a same-night ticket. The [plumbing version of this problem](/blog/answering-service-for-plumbers) is brutal in a short window. Construction is slower and larger.

The GC who cannot reach you awards the work to whoever answered. The homeowner who got three bids last week calls the first company that picks up to schedule. The inspector on site who needs a decision in the next twenty minutes leaves a voicemail you will hear at lunch, after the window closed.

You already know the pattern because you live it:

**The phone rings when both hands are occupied.** On a ladder, in a trench, inside a dusty remodel with earpro on, pouring a slab. The crew cannot stop for a bid call. The person calling will not wait for a callback that arrives three hours later.

**A lot of the valuable calls are not emergencies.** They are bid requests, change orders, and "can you start Monday." They still walk if nobody answers. After-hours still matters: storm damage, a leak in a house you have open, a fence that came down. That is a separate product question. See [after hours answering service](/blog/after-hours-answering-service) if nights are the actual hole.

**The mix on the line is messy.** Homeowners, GCs, property managers, suppliers, inspectors, and the lumberyard. A per-minute plan bills all of that the same. A service that cannot tell a bid from a delivery confirmation will fill your afternoon with junk and still charge you for it.

That is why a generic "we take messages" pitch is a poor fit. The script has to know construction, or you are paying someone to be slightly faster voicemail.

## The three kinds of construction answering service

Search results for this keyword mix three products. Sort them before you take a sales call.

### 1. A traditional live answering service

A room of operators answers with your company name, follows a script, and emails or texts you a message.

They sound human. They can follow an escalation rule if you write one: "if the caller says the inspector is waiting, call the super now." They do not know a change order from a cold bid unless the script says so. On a per-minute plan you pay for hold time, wrong numbers, and the four-minute supplier call about a back-ordered window.

Ask how non-customer calls are billed. Ask who is on the line at 6am. Those two answers decide whether the invoice is tolerable.

### 2. A virtual receptionist

Further upmarket: fewer people, a longer script, more authority. The published ones will book a site visit on a calendar and take a deposit if you pay for those add-ons.

This is the polished front desk. It is also the expensive option per answered call, and after-hours is the part to interrogate. Plenty of firms run a strong daytime team and a different overflow night team. Construction's useful calls sit on both sides of 5pm, so "24/7" on the sales page is not the same as "the same people at 6:15am."

Ruby and Smith.ai, priced below, sit in this band.

### 3. An AI receptionist

A voice AI answers, holds a conversation from information you uploaded, asks your intake questions, and sends you the lead — transcript, summary, text.

It picks up on the first ring at 6am, on a Sunday, on a holiday pour. The monthly cost does not jump because March was busy. What it cannot do, on most products, is book a calendar, take a card, or type into Jobber, Buildertrend, or ServiceTitan while the caller is still talking. If those three things are the job, you are buying live. If the job is "answer it, qualify it, get it to me," AI is usually the cheaper tool.

The failure mode is improvising. An AI that invents a start date or a price you never gave it will lose the GC and earn you a review. The question to ask is what it does when it does not know. Hand-off to a human is the acceptable answer. Guessing is not.

The category write-up is in [AI receptionist for small business](/blog/ai-receptionist-for-small-business). Pricing shapes for AI vs live are in [virtual receptionist pricing](/blog/virtual-receptionist-pricing).

:::callout
**Ask this of any AI before you forward the truck number.**
"What does it do when it doesn't know the answer?" If the answer is anything other than stop and hand off, keep walking. A confident wrong start date is worse than a missed call.
:::

Two traps show up on almost every construction invoice.

**Per-minute maths that only works in January.** A slow month looks cheap. A month you actually won work looks expensive, because every supplier follow-up and every "did my check clear" call is a minute. Model the month you are pouring three jobs, not the month you are bidding.

**"Unlimited" with a fair-use clause.** There is always a clause. You will meet it the week a storm hits and the phone does not stop.

Hiring a person instead is a different decision. A receptionist covers one shift at a desk. Construction's missed calls happen on the other side of that shift: 6am, lunch on a noisy site, 6:30pm when the homeowner gets home. Hire if you also need a body in the office. Do not hire to solve the truck-phone problem.

### A one-crew example

Say you take **60 inbound calls a month** that are actually about work, plus another pile of suppliers and spam, and a missed awarded bid is a five-figure job, not a $400 truck roll.

- A **$300/month** live plan has to save you a sliver of one awarded job a year to pay for itself, if the intake is good enough that you can call back with a number.
- A **$250/month** 50-minute Ruby plan is roughly **$5 a minute**. Two long supplier calls and a couple of hold-times will chew a noticeable hole in that bucket.
- Missing **one** GC award because the saw was running wipes out either invoice for a long time.

The comparison that matters is not Ruby vs Smith. It is the answering bill vs one job you did not get to bid. Most owners already have that number in their head. Write it down before the sales call.

## What the person answering should actually capture

Brand matters less than the script. For a construction line, five answers decide whether you can call back with a price or you are starting from zero:

:::steps
New job or existing job? :: A change order on a house you already have open is a different callback than a cold bid. Get this first.
What's the address or job site? :: Travel time and whether you even take work in that zip code. Also the only way the super knows which site if two are running.
What kind of work, and when do you need someone? :: Framing next Monday is a crew question. A bathroom quote for next month is a Saturday estimate. "ASAP" is not a date — make them say one.
Who is calling? :: Homeowner, GC, property manager, or inspector. Each one has a different approval path and a different patience level.
Is anything unsafe right now? :: Open to weather, active leak, structural, temp fence down. That is the branch that should ring a person immediately instead of sitting in a lead list.
:::

A name and a callback number is a message. Those five answers are a job you can accept, decline, or price from the cab on the way to the next site.

If nobody picked up at all, a fast text back still beats silence. That mechanic is [missed call text back](/blog/missed-call-text-back).

## What to ask before you sign

Six questions, in the order they will save you money:

1. **"Who answers at 6:15am Monday and at 7pm Saturday, and is that the same team as Tuesday at 10?"** Construction's useful calls sit in those two windows. If the honest answer is a different, cheaper team, you are buying two products.
2. **"Walk me through a GC awarding a job, and walk me through an inspector on site."** You want the actual path — questions asked, who gets the text, how fast. Vague here stays vague on the call.
3. **"How do you bill a supplier, a robocall, or a hang-up?"** On per-minute and per-call plans this is real money. Smith.ai's live plans, as of 10 September 2026, say spam does not count if caller ID passes. Ask every other vendor the same question and get it in writing.
4. **"Can the person answering tell a bid from a change order from a delivery?"** If they cannot, you will spend the afternoon sorting their notes.
5. **"What do I actually receive when the call ends?"** A transcript and a summary on your phone is usable on site. A paragraph in an email you will open at 9pm is how leads die.
6. **"What's the cancellation term?"** Annual lock-ins are common. Smith.ai live is month-to-month on the page we checked. Confirm it anyway.

Do not skip the 6:15am question. A construction answering service that is excellent at 10am and outsourced at dawn is two products sold as one.

Keep your existing number. Forward the line on the trucks and the Google listing so nothing on the wrap changes. Forwarding carries voice. Texts stay behind unless you port. If you want a second, cleaner line for ads, a [toll free number for business](/blog/toll-free-number-for-business) is the usual add, not a replacement for the number people already have.

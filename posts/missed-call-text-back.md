Saturday, 2:17 p.m. You are on a roof in July with both hands on a flashing. The phone is in the truck with the doors shut. It rings twice and stops. By the time you climb down, the missed-call banner is twenty minutes old and that number is already trying the next listing.

Unless a text left your business line about thirty seconds after the ring died.

"This is Dana at Oak Street Roofing — sorry I missed you. What's going on with the roof?"

The homeowner types two sentences from the driveway. You still have a job to quote when you get off the ladder.

:::callout
**The text is a net, not the whole phone system.**
It catches the person who will not leave a voicemail. It does not replace answering the call, and it does not dispatch a truck. Judge it on one thing: does a stranger who just hung up know who you are and what to do next.
:::

## The short answer

**Missed call text back** is an automatic SMS to whoever just rang you and did not get through. The system reads the caller ID, waits until the call is missed, and sends a short message from your business number. If two-way texting is on, their reply lands in the same inbox you already use.

**What to write:** your name and business, that you saw the call, one question they can answer in a sentence, and how to reach you. Four lines is plenty. "Your call is important to us" is wasted space.

**How to turn it on:** a business number that can send SMS, one saved template, and a rule that fires on an unanswered call. In the US, outbound business texts wait on a one-time carrier registration that takes a few business days. Incoming calls do not wait on that step.

**What it will not do:** book a calendar, take a card, or talk a panicking homeowner through a shut-off. For a burst pipe at 11pm, they still want a voice. The text is what you send when you could not be that voice.

## What it is

A missed call used to mean a voicemail, or nothing. Most callers pick nothing. They hang up on the second ring and dial the next result. The banner on your lock screen is a record of a conversation that already ended.

Text-back changes the last step. The same missed call still happens. Instead of hoping they leave a message, your number sends them a line they can answer with their thumbs.

Three versions of this exist, and they are not interchangeable.

- **You, by hand.** You see the missed call, you type a text from your cell. Free. Slow. Easy to forget when the next job starts.
- **A rule on a business phone.** The number on the van sends the SMS the moment the call dies. Same wording every time. Works while you are on a ladder.
- **A live answering service.** A person picks up, then texts or emails you the notes. That is a different product with a different bill. The cost shapes are in [what an answering service costs](/blog/answering-service-cost), and the vendor walk-through is in [the best answering services for small business](/blog/best-answering-service-for-small-business).

This page is about the middle one: the automatic text that goes out when nobody could pick up.

## What happens after the ring dies

The mechanic is short. Knowing it stops you from buying the wrong setting.

:::steps
The call hits your business number :: Not your personal cell, if you can help it. The number on the van, the invoice, and the Google listing.
Your phones ring :: One phone, a ring group, a forward to your cell. Same idea.
Nobody answers :: Timeout, decline, or you are already on another call. The system treats all three as a miss.
It reads the caller ID :: That is the number the text will go to. If there is no caller ID, there is no text.
It sends your template :: From the business number, so the thread looks like it belongs to the company they meant to call.
A reply opens a thread :: You answer as the business. On a team line, that thread should live in one inbox so two people do not text the same customer.
:::

Two settings decide whether this helps or annoys people.

**When it fires.** After hours only is the timid version. A one-person shop misses calls at 10am too — you are under a sink, in a crawlspace, on a mower. Fire on every unanswered call, including the one that came in while you were already talking.

**What voicemail does.** If the recording says "leave a message" and the text says "reply here," you have given them two jobs. Make the voicemail point at the text. "I just sent you a text from this number. Reply there and I'll get it." Then shut up.

The delay should be seconds, not minutes. A text that arrives after they have already hired the next plumber is a receipt for a job you lost.

## Why a text beats a voicemail for a trade

Voicemail asks a wet, angry, or busy person to give a speech to a machine. A lot of them will not. A text asks for one sentence they can send from the driveway.

That matters more in the trades than it does in an office, because the expensive calls arrive when you cannot take them. [An answering service for plumbers](/blog/answering-service-for-plumbers) is built around that 11pm emergency. The same pattern shows up for HVAC, electrical, cleaning, and anyone else whose customers call from a problem, not from a desk.

[Angi's emergency plumber guide](https://www.angi.com/articles/emergency-plumber-cost.htm) (updated 9 July 2026) puts after-hours labour at **$100 to $500 an hour**, averaging about **$170**, with a separate trip fee of **$150 to $300** on many tickets. One recovered Saturday call pays for a long time of texts. One missed one wipes out the same stretch.

A text will not triage a burst line the way a voice conversation will. It will keep the person who was about to hang up from disappearing.

Three habits make it work in a trade:

**Send it from the business number.** A text from your personal cell looks like a stranger. A text from the number they just dialled looks like a callback.

**Ask a useful question.** "How can I help you today?" is a blank page. "Is water running right now?" or "Is this for a quote or a repair?" gives them something to answer in five words.

**Read the thread before you drive.** A name and a number is a message. An address, a symptom, and whether it is a rental is a job you can price.

## Copy you can send today

Steal these. Change the name. Keep them short. If you catch yourself adding a second paragraph, delete it.

**On a job, could not pick up**

> Hey, this is Dana at Oak Street Roofing. Just missed your call — on a roof and couldn't pick up. What's going on? Reply here or call this number back.

**After hours, you will see it in the morning**

> This is Oak Street Roofing. We just missed your call. We're closed for the night. Tell me what's going on and I'll call you first thing. If it's an emergency, reply EMERGENCY.

Only use that last line if you actually look. A fake emergency button trains people to ignore you.

**Emergency trade (plumbing, HVAC, electrical)**

> This is Mike at Ridge Plumbing. Sorry I missed you. Is water running right now, and do you know where the shut-off is? Reply here and I'll call you back as soon as I can get to the phone.

**Quote request**

> Thanks for calling Ridge Plumbing — I saw I missed you. Looking for a quote? Reply with the address and a short description and I'll get back to you today.

**Someone who already hired you**

> Hey, it's Mike at Ridge. Just missed your call. Everything okay at the house? Text me here.

**You are with a customer and will call in a set window**

> This is Dana at Oak Street. With a customer and couldn't pick up. I can call you back after 4. If it's faster, tell me here what you need.

Pick one default. Save it. The 2pm-on-a-roof version of you will not write anything better than the version of you sitting at the kitchen table.

### What belongs in every text

Four pieces, in this order:

1. **Who you are.** First name and business. The caller has already forgotten which listing they dialled.
2. **That you saw the call.** So they know this is a reply, not a cold text.
3. **One question.** Symptom, address, quote vs repair, emergency or not. One.
4. **How to reach you.** "Reply here" is enough if the thread is live. Add "or call this number" if you want the option.

Leave out the mission statement, the hours paragraph, the link to a form, and any promise of a time you cannot keep. "I'll be there in 20" from a crawlspace is how you get a one-star review.

If you serve Spanish-speaking customers, write a second template and set the rule to match the listing they called. Do not run the English one through a translator on the truck.

## How to set it up

The DIY path is a note on your phone and a habit. It works until the afternoon you forget. The durable path is a rule.

:::steps
Put the public number on a line that can text :: The number on the van has to be the number that sends the SMS. Forwarding a landline to your cell carries the call, not the texting identity.
Write one template and stop :: Name, missed-you, one question, reply here. Save it in the phone system, not in your head.
Set the miss rule :: Unanswered after a few rings, busy, declined, after hours. For a solo operator, all of those should fire.
Decide what voicemail says :: Point it at the text. Do not ask for a long message you will not listen to until tonight.
Register the number for business SMS :: In the US this is a carrier step, usually called A2P 10DLC. Plan on a few business days. Calls and voicemail do not wait on it.
Test from a second phone :: Call yourself, let it ring out, read the text like a stranger. Then try a landline. Then try a number that looks like spam.
:::

## The parts people skip

**Carrier registration.** Personal texts from your cell do not need this. Business texts from a business number do. The carriers require a one-time registration before outbound SMS will send. Incoming calls are unrelated. If a salesperson tells you texts start the same afternoon you sign up, ask them which carrier they think they are.

**Landlines.** Some of the numbers that ring you cannot receive SMS. The text fails, or it goes to an email-to-text gateway the caller never checks. Keep the missed-call log. Dial those back with your voice.

**Spam and robocalls.** A rule that texts every missed caller will also text the junk. That costs a little money and looks sloppy if a person ever sees it. Filter what you can. Do not obsess. A few wasted texts a month is cheaper than a rule so tight it misses the real ones.

**Double messages.** Auto-text plus a voicemail transcription plus an "AI follow-up" is three pings in a minute. Pick one path. The caller should get a single, clear next step.

**Consent and marketing.** A short text about the call they placed is usually treated as part of that inquiry. A coupon, a review ask, or "join our list" is a different message and a different set of rules. The [TCPA](https://www.fcc.gov/sites/default/files/tcpa-rules.pdf) is the federal starting point (FCC; the current unofficial compilation is the one to read, and it changes). State rules sit on top. This page is not legal advice. If you want to send anything promotional, get consent in writing and have someone who does this for a living read your wording.

**STOP.** If you run an ongoing text program, honour opt-outs. For a one-off "sorry I missed you," keep the message about that call and leave it there.

## When the text is not enough

Some callers will not type. Some jobs cannot wait for a thread.

A homeowner standing in water wants a person on the line, not a chat. So does someone whose heat died in January. So does an angry customer. The text can still go out. It should not be the only thing that happens on those calls.

That is the line between this feature and an actual receptionist, human or AI. Missed-call text back is what you want when the call already died. If you can pick up, or have something that picks up, do that first.

A live service is the other fork, if you need a person to book a calendar or take a deposit while the caller is still talking. Price that against a month of your real volume, not the headline rate, and test the 11pm escalation before you sign.

For everyone else, the order is simple. Answer when you can. Text in seconds when you cannot. Call back the ones who cannot receive a text. Write the template on a calm day, not from the roof.

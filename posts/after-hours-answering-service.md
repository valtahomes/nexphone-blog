It is 9:12 on a Thursday night. A breaker will not reset. The homeowner has a dark kitchen, a fridge full of food, and three electricians saved in their phone. They start at the top.

First rings four times. Voicemail. They hang up before the beep. Second is a menu — press 1 for service, press 2 for billing. They hang up on “press 1.” Third picks up on the second ring, takes the address, and a van is rolling twenty minutes later.

That third company did not have a better Google ranking. They had someone, or something, answering after 6pm.

:::callout
**The only test that matters is the 11pm call.**
Any answering service can take a name during lunch. After-hours coverage is what happens when you are at dinner, on a roof, or asleep — whether the call gets triaged, whether it reaches you in time, and what a month of that actually costs.
:::

## The short answer

An after-hours answering service picks up your business line when you are closed. That can mean evenings only, weekends and holidays, or the whole 24 hours. Some services are a live call centre. Some are a virtual receptionist working from your script. Some are a voice AI that answers on the first ring and passes the job to you.

**What it costs:** live after-hours coverage commonly lands between **$250 and $800 a month** once nights and the quiet junk calls are in the bill. Per-call live plans start around **$300 a month for 30 calls**. Flat-rate AI answering sits roughly **$20–$200 a month** and does not add a night surcharge. Prices move, so treat those as shapes, and always price a busy month, not a quiet one.

**What you are buying:** the minutes between the caller saying “the power is out” and your phone buzzing. A message in your email at 8am is voicemail with extra steps.

**What to judge it on:** who answers at 11pm on a Sunday, whether they can tell an emergency from a nuisance, and how you get notified. Everything else is packaging.

## What “after hours” means

People search *after hours answering service* for three different setups. Vendors mix the language on purpose.

**Nights and weekends only.** You answer during the day. After 5pm, on Saturday, and on holidays, calls forward to the service. This is the common setup for a one-truck plumber, HVAC tech, electrician, or cleaner who is the front desk until they clock off.

**Always on, with you as the overflow.** The service answers first, all day. You take the calls they transfer. Useful if you are on a job site and cannot reach the phone even at 10am.

**True 24/7, same product at 2am.** The sales page says 24/7. The question is whether the 2am operator is the same team that answered at 10am, or a cheaper overflow desk with a thinner script.

Those three products cost different amounts and fail in different places. If you only miss calls after 6pm, paying for a full-day receptionist is wasted money. If your emergencies arrive at 11pm, an “after hours” plan that emails you in the morning is also wasted money.

The mechanics are usually simple. You keep the number on the van and the Google listing. After close, you forward that line to the service. Forwarding carries the call. It does not carry texts. If you want the same number to text customers, the number has to be ported, not forwarded.

## Why after-hours calls are a different kind of missed call

A missed call at 2pm often calls back. A missed call at 9pm usually does not. The person on the other end is standing in a dark kitchen, a hot bedroom, or an inch of water, and they have a list. You are one name on it.

[Angi's 2026 emergency plumber guide](https://www.angi.com/articles/emergency-plumber-cost.htm) (updated 9 July 2026) puts after-hours labour at **$100 to $500 an hour**, averaging about **$170**, with holiday rates up to **$600** and a separate trip fee of **$150 to $300**. That is the ticket walking out the door when the phone rings four times and dies in voicemail. HVAC after-hours call-outs and electrician night jobs sit in the same neighbourhood: premium labour, trip fee, someone who will not wait until Monday.

Three things make nights harder than days.

**You cannot take the call.** Hands in a panel, on a ladder, wet, or you have already gone home. The window the caller will stay on the line is a couple of rings.

**The urgent ones are worth the most.** A no-cool call on a Saturday in July is a premium dispatch. A filter change next Thursday is not. The calls you are least able to answer are the ones with the highest ticket. The same pattern shows up in [plumbing](/blog/answering-service-for-plumbers) and [HVAC](/blog/hvac-answering-service). The 11pm job is the one that pays for the month.

**Callers will not sit in a menu.** Somebody watching a sump pump lose the fight does not want to press 1. They want a sentence within a couple of rings. A recording, a hold loop, or “please leave a message” and they dial the next result.

Property managers live this in a slightly different shape: a tenant with a lockout or a burst pipe at 1am. The economics are the same. Coverage that cannot escalate at night is not coverage. The property-management version of this decision is in [answering service for property management](/blog/answering-service-for-property-management).

## The three kinds of after-hours answering

### 1. A live call centre

A room of operators answers as your business, follows a script, takes a message, and emails or texts it to you. Some will call your on-call tech if you write that into the script.

They sound human. They can handle a spike of calls. They will follow an escalation rule if you wrote one clearly and the night operator actually uses it.

They do not know your trade. The person on the line has never stood in a crawlspace. If the caller says something the script did not anticipate, the answer is usually “someone will get back to you.” On a Thursday at 10am that is annoying. On a Thursday at 10pm that is a lost job.

Watch who is on the night shift. A lot of centres run a smaller, cheaper overflow team after 8pm. Ask the Sunday-night question before you sign, not after the first missed emergency.

### 2. A virtual receptionist

Same idea, further upmarket: a smaller team, a longer script, more authority. Some will book a calendar and take a card. That is the product a lot of professional offices want.

It is also the expensive option per call, and after-hours is where the product quietly changes. Daytime may be a named receptionist. Nighttime is often a different desk. If you are buying this for the 11pm call, ask who answers at 2am, by name of team, and listen to a recorded sample. [Virtual receptionist pricing](/blog/virtual-receptionist-pricing) is the live-receptionist version of that maths.

### 3. An AI receptionist

A voice AI answers the line, holds a conversation, answers from information you gave it, takes the job details, and passes the lead to you. It picks up on the first ring at 3am on Christmas Day for a flat monthly cost that does not jump because you had a busy Saturday.

It does not have an off night and it does not swap in a weaker team after dark. That is the actual after-hours argument for AI, and it is a real one.

Where it should stop is judgement. An upset customer, a complaint, a situation it was not told about. Those need a person. An AI that always produces an answer is worse than voicemail. The category breakdown, including who this is for, lives in [AI receptionist for small business](/blog/ai-receptionist-for-small-business).

Most AI answering products do not book a calendar, take a card, or type into Jobber, Housecall Pro, or ServiceTitan while the caller is still on the line. If those three things are the job, you are buying a live service. If the job is “answer the 11pm call, qualify it, and get it to me,” AI is usually the cheaper tool.

:::callout
**Ask this of any after-hours provider, live or AI:**
“What does it do when it doesn’t know the answer?” If the honest answer is anything other than “it stops and hands off to a human,” keep looking. Improvising at 11pm is how a recoverable call becomes a one-star review.
:::

**Hiring a night person instead**

A second body on the phone for nights is a wage, overtime, and the Saturday the pipe bursts while they are off. The [Bureau of Labor Statistics *Occupational Outlook Handbook*](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/construction-and-extraction/plumbers-pipefitters-and-steamfitters.htm) (last modified 28 August 2025) puts the median plumber wage at **$62,970 a year, $30.27 an hour**, from May 2024 data. That is one licensed person, on one shift. Hiring is the right move if you also need a human at a desk. It is the wrong move if the job is “answer the 11pm call.”

Two traps that show up more at night than during the day.

**Per-minute maths on emergency calls.** A panicked homeowner talks. Hold time counts. The four-minute burst-pipe call is the one you bought the service for, and it is the one that burns the plan. Model a hot Saturday, not a Tuesday in March.

**“24/7” with a different night desk.** If after-hours is a separate, cheaper team, you have two products. The cheap one is the one that will handle the expensive call.

### A one-truck example

Say you take **80 inbound calls a month**, half of them after 5pm, and a typical after-hours dispatch, labour plus trip, lands around **$400**. That is conservative against Angi's ranges.

- A **$300/month** live plan has to save you slightly less than one emergency a month to pay for itself.
- A **$80/month** flat AI plan has to save you one emergency every five months.
- Missing **one** Saturday no-cool or burst-pipe because nobody picked up wipes out either invoice.

The comparison that matters is between a month of coverage and one missed night job. Most owners already know their number for this. Write it down before you take the sales call.

## What should happen on the 11pm call

Ignore the feature list for a minute. Walk the call.

:::steps
The line rings after you close :: Pickup should be within a couple of rings. No menu, no “please hold for the next operator,” no voicemail greeting that tells a flooded kitchen to call back at 8.
They find out if it is an emergency :: Water running, power out, no heat in January, no cool with infants in the house — those are truck-out-now. A dripping tap, a quote for next week, a parts question — those are a morning callback. Somebody has to ask.
They take enough to dispatch :: Name, number, address, access notes, and the one sentence that tells you whether to roll tonight. A name and a callback number is a message, not a lead.
You hear about it immediately :: A text or a phone ping, while the caller is still deciding. Email that lands in a digest at 7am is how after-hours services fail.
The odd cases get a human :: Complaints, “your tech was just here and it is worse,” anything off the script. Those should not be guessed at.
:::

If any of those five steps is “we email you a transcript,” you have a slightly nicer voicemail.

Missed-call hang-ups are a separate hole. A lot of after-hours callers drop at ring four and never leave a message. A [missed-call text-back](/blog/missed-call-text-back) that fires in seconds can still catch that person while they are looking at the next listing. Answering and text-back do different jobs. You may want both.

## What to ask before you sign

Six questions, in the order they will save you money.

1. **“Who answers at 11pm on a Sunday, and are they the same team as at 10am Tuesday?”** For a lot of providers the honest answer is no. That is the call you are buying.
2. **“Do nights, weekends, or holidays cost extra?”** Some live services include them. Some do not. Get it in the quote, not the FAQ.
3. **“Walk me through an emergency. What do you ask, and how do I hear about it?”** You want a specific path — questions, threshold, and the channel that hits your phone. Vague here will be vague at 11pm too.
4. **“How do you bill a wrong number, a robocall, or a hang-up?”** On per-minute and per-call plans this is real money, and after-hours lines collect a lot of junk.
5. **“What do I actually receive when the call ends?”** A transcript and a summary you can act on, or a paragraph you have to re-key. Ask where it lands — text, app, email — and whether that channel is one you will see in bed.
6. **“What’s the cancellation term?”** Annual lock-ins are common. Month-to-month is worth paying a little more for until you have tested a real Saturday night.

Then test it. Do not skip this.

Call your own line after 9pm from a number that is not in your contacts. Play an emergency. Play a “can you come Tuesday.” Play something the script does not cover: a complaint, a weird access issue, a landlord who has to approve the spend. Time the gap between hang-up and your phone buzzing. If that gap is measured in hours, keep shopping.

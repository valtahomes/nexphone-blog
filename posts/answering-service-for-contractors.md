It is 6:47 on a Thursday. You are on a second-story addition, the compressor is running, the homeowner is asking about the window trim, and your phone is in the truck. Across town, a different homeowner — the one you quoted last Tuesday — is calling to say they want to move forward this weekend if you can start. They will try two other contractors before they leave a voicemail.

They will not wait until you are off the ladder. The contractor who answers that call gets a job worth several thousand dollars and a customer who will call again when the kitchen is next.

## Why contractors lose the calls that pay

Most small businesses miss a call and lose a chatty inquiry. Contractors miss a call and lose a bid they already spent two hours writing, which is a different kind of hole in the month.

The phone rings when you cannot take it. You are on a roof, in a client's house with a saw running, in a permit office, or driving between sites with a passenger who is still deciding on tile. The window in which that caller is still choosing a contractor is short. They have three other numbers from Google and a brother-in-law who "knows a guy."

The expensive calls arrive after 5pm. A homeowner walks the job after work, finds the quote on the fridge, and dials. Storm damage, a leaking skylight the crew left, a lock-box that will not open: those come at night and on Saturday. The calls you are least able to answer are the ones with the highest ticket.

Callers will not sit in a menu. Somebody standing in a half-finished bathroom does not want to press 2 for sales. They want a sentence within a couple of rings. A recording, a hold loop, or a "please leave a message after the tone" and they hang up.

An [after hours answering service](/blog/after-hours-answering-service) is only worth the invoice if it is good at exactly that moment, which is why the escalation rules matter more than the sales page.

Plumbing, HVAC, and electrical shops live this same problem with a sharper emergency edge. The trade-specific version for plumbing is in [answering service for plumbers](/blog/answering-service-for-plumbers). HVAC is in [hvac answering service](/blog/hvac-answering-service). General contractors get a mix: fewer burst pipes, more "can you start Monday" and "the crew left and the back door will not latch." The answering product still has to tell those two apart.

## The three kinds of answering service for contractors

### 1. A traditional live answering service

A room of operators answers as your company, follows a script you supply, takes a message, and emails or texts it to you.

They sound human and can handle a burst of calls. They will follow an escalation rule if you write one clearly: "if the caller says the building is open, text the on-call number until someone replies."

They do not know your jobs. The operator has never stood in that kitchen. They have never met the client on Oak Street. If the caller says something the script did not anticipate, the usual line is "someone will get back to you," which is what the caller already assumed.

Watch the billing. Per-minute pricing means you pay for hold time, for the lumberyard, for the tool-rental confirmation, and for the four-minute call that was a lead-gen spam shop. Ask how they bill non-customer calls before you sign.

### 2. A virtual receptionist

Same idea, further upmarket: a smaller team, sometimes a named person, working from a longer script with more authority. Some will book a slot on a calendar and take a card over the phone.

The front-desk impression is stronger. Complex intake is stronger. The price is also higher per call, and after-hours is the catch. A lot of these products staff a polished team at 10am and a different, cheaper overflow team at 7pm, which is the call you were buying coverage for. Ask who answers at 2am, by name of the team, not by a slogan.

### 3. An AI receptionist

A voice AI answers the line, holds a conversation, answers from information you gave it, takes the job details, and passes the lead to you.

It picks up on the first ring, at 3am, on Christmas, for a monthly cost that does not punish a busy week. It does not have a shift change and it does not decide your Saturday call is somebody else's problem.

Judgement is the failure mode. An AI that always produces an answer is worse than voicemail. A client calling to complain about a punch list, or a homeowner describing a situation you have never seen, needs a person. A confident wrong answer costs you the review and the remaining draws on the job.

Most AI answering products do not book a calendar, take a card, or type into Jobber, Housecall Pro, or ServiceTitan while the caller is still talking. If those three things are the job, you are buying a live service. If the job is "answer the 6:47pm call, find out if it is a bid or a job-site problem, and get it to me," AI is usually the cheaper tool. ## What an answering service for contractors costs

Pricing in this category is hard to compare because the three models hide cost in different places. The industry-wide breakdown lives in [what an answering service costs](/blog/answering-service-cost). The contractor version is the same shapes, with a warning: bid calls run long, and supplier calls look like customer calls to a per-minute clock.

**Per minute (live)**

- Shape: a base fee plus minutes. Ruby's published rungs on 8 September 2026 start at **$250 for 50 minutes**. That is **$5 a minute** if you use the bundle exactly, less per minute as you buy up.
- You pay more when: calls run long, or volume climbs.
- Watch: hold time, wrong numbers, robocalls, and the lumberyard, all billed as minutes. Rounding up to the full minute is common.

**Per call (live)**

- Shape: Smith.ai Starter at **$300 for 30 calls** is **$10 a call** inside the bundle, then **$11.50** over. Bigger bundles drop the overage toward **$8.50**.
- You pay more when: volume climbs.
- Watch: what counts as a "call." Smith.ai's live plans state they do not bill spam when Caller ID passes. Ask every other per-call vendor the same question in writing.

**Flat monthly (usually AI)**

- Shape: a monthly rate that does not move with every extra ring. Some AI products still meter calls or minutes inside that rate — Smith.ai AI Pro is a hybrid, **$150 plus $2.00 a call** inside 75.
- You pay more when: you blow a cap, or you add the live-agent safety net.
- Watch: whether there is a minutes or calls cap, what overage costs, and whether spam counts.

**Hiring instead**

A person at a desk can do things an answering service will not: greet walk-ins, chase subs, and sit with a set of plans. They also work one shift, take lunch, and are not on the line at 7pm Saturday. Hiring is the right move if you also need a body in the office. It is the wrong move if the job is "answer the phone while I am on a roof."

Two traps show up on almost every sales call.

**"Unlimited" with a fair-use clause.** If a plan says unlimited anything, find that clause. You will meet it in the month a storm hits and every past client calls about tarps.

**Per-minute maths that only works when you are quiet.** A per-minute plan looks cheap on a slow February and expensive in a good June, which means the answering bill rises exactly when you are busiest and have the least time to audit it. Model a busy month, not an average one.

## Dispatch, for a contractor, is not what the brochure means

"Dispatch" on an answering-service sales page usually means: we will take the details and notify you. It does not mean they will call your carpenter, your plumber, and your electrician, give the homeowner an ETA, and update the job board.

For a one-crew or two-crew shop, that is fine — you *are* dispatch. You need the right facts fast, and you need the phone to buzz while you can still return the call the same evening.

What you want from the service:

**Triage.** Existing job vs new work. Unsafe / unsecured building vs "can you look at a deck next month." Those are different nights for you.

**A path to you that works with dirty hands.** A text with the address, the callback number, and a two-line summary beats a voicemail you will listen to at 9pm. A live transfer is better on a true emergency, worse when you are on a roof and cannot talk.

**A stop rule.** If they cannot reach you, what does the caller hear. "We will try the owner now" is honest. "A technician is on the way" is a lie unless you have already said yes.

Live services can attempt a sequence: try your cell, then a partner, then a voicemail, if you write that sequence down. AI services text you the lead and can escalate when the script says so. Neither one should invent an arrival time.

If you need someone to book the walkthrough into a calendar and take a deposit while the caller is still on the line, that is a live receptionist with those add-ons switched on. Budget for it. Ruby lists scheduling and payment collection in the base feature set. Smith.ai live bills them per call. Do not buy a message-taking plan and hope dispatch appears later.

## What to ask before you sign

Six questions, in the order they will save you money:

1. **"Who answers at 7pm on a Saturday, and is it the same team as at 10am Tuesday?"** For a lot of providers the honest answer is no. That Saturday call is the one you are buying.
2. **"Walk me through an after-hours job-site call."** You want the questions they ask, the threshold for "get the owner now," and how you are reached. If it is vague in the sales call, it will be vague at 7pm too.
3. **"How do you bill a supplier, a wrong number, a robocall, or a hang-up?"** On per-minute and per-call plans this is real money. Contractors get more vendor traffic than a typical office.
4. **"Can the person answering tell an active job from a new bid?"** "I'm the kitchen on Oak" is not a new lead. If your service treats every caller as a fresh estimate, you will look disorganised to people already writing you cheques.
5. **"What do I actually receive when the call ends?"** A transcript and a text you can act on from the truck is useful. A morning email digest of last night's calls is how jobs go to the next name on Google.
6. **"What's the cancellation term?"** Annual lock-ins are common. Smith.ai live and AI both describe month-to-month on the pages checked 8 September 2026, with a 30-day notice on the AI product. Ask everyone else the same way.

Do not skip the Saturday question. A contractor answering service that is excellent at 10am and outsourced at 7pm is two different products sold as one.

## The four questions your answering service should ask every caller

Whatever you buy, the script is worth more than the logo. For a contracting company, four answers decide the night:

:::steps
Is this about a job already in progress, or new work? :: An active client with a lock-box problem is a different call from a stranger with a Pinterest kitchen. Get this first.
Is anyone hurt, or is the building unsecured — water, open door, tarp, power? :: That is the truck-out-now pile. Everything else can wait until you are off the ladder.
What's the address, and is it the same site we already know? :: Travel time decides whether tonight is even possible. "The kitchen on Oak" only helps if the person answering can connect it to Oak.
When do you need someone there? :: "This weekend if you can start" is a bid about to be awarded. "Whenever you have an opening in October" is a list. Those two should not land in the same text.
:::

An answering service that collects a name and a number has handed you a message. One that collects those four answers has handed you a job you can price, decline, or roll on before you get back in the truck.

Optional fifth, if insurance work is part of your book: is this a claim, and is there a claim number. It changes who has to approve the spend.

Train the script on your actual jobs for a week. Then call your own line at 7pm from a number that is not in your contacts and try to sound like a tired homeowner. That test is worth more than a demo.

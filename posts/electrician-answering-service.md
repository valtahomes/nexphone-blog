It is 10:17 on a Sunday night. A homeowner smells burning at a bedroom outlet, the faceplate is discolored, they have pulled the plug, and they are standing in the hallway with a flashlight, calling every electrician on the first page of Google.

They will not leave a voicemail. They will not wait until morning if the next listing picks up. The shop that answers that call gets an after-hours ticket and a customer who remembers who showed up in the dark.

:::callout
**The only test that matters is after hours.**
Any answering service can take a name during lunch. What you are paying for is the call that comes in while both your hands are in a panel — whether it gets triaged, whether it reaches you, and what a month of that coverage actually costs.
:::

## Why electrical calls are a bad miss

A missed after-hours electrical call is a premium ticket walking to the next name on Google. The caller is in the dark, or they think something is about to catch fire. They are not comparison shopping. They are working down a list.

Three things make the trade punishing.

**You cannot take the phone when it matters.** You are in a panel, in an attic, on a lift, or your hands are not free. The window in which that homeowner is still deciding who to hire is measured in minutes.

**The urgent ones pay the most.** A sparking outlet at 10pm is an after-hours rate. A dimmer that flickers next Thursday is not. The calls hardest to answer are the ones with the highest ticket.

**Callers hang up on a menu.** Somebody who smells burning does not want to press 1 for service. They want a sentence within a couple of rings. A recording sends them to the next result.

That is the same pattern we wrote about for [an answering service for plumbers](/blog/answering-service-for-plumbers), and it shows up again in [HVAC answering service](/blog/hvac-answering-service) coverage. The product you buy has to work at the moment you are least able to pick up.

There is a fourth problem that is more electrical than plumbing. A bad answer can be dangerous. A human or AI operator who tells a caller to reset a breaker while the panel is hot, or to “just check the connections,” has left you with a liability problem on top of a missed job. The service should triage and pass the lead. It should not diagnose.

## The three kinds of answering service for electricians

### 1. A traditional live answering service

A room of operators answers as your company, follows a script you supply, takes a message, and emails or texts it to you.

**Good at:** sounding human, handling a burst of volume after a storm, following an escalation rule if you wrote one.
**Bad at:** knowing anything about electrical work. The operator has never opened a panel. They read your script. If the caller says something the script did not anticipate, you usually get “someone will get back to you.”

Watch the billing. Per-minute pricing means you pay for hold time, wrong numbers, telemarketers, and the four-minute call that turned out to be a supplier. Ask how they bill non-customer calls before you sign.

### 2. A virtual receptionist

Same idea, further upmarket: a smaller team, sometimes a named person, working from a longer script with more authority. Some will book a calendar and take a card.

**Good at:** the polished front-desk impression, longer intake, transferring a live call.
**Bad at:** the price, and the after-hours bait-and-switch. Plenty of these services are excellent at 10am and a different, cheaper overflow team at 11pm, which is the call you were buying. Ask who answers at 2am, by name of the team, not the brand.

If you want a dedicated look at that model, [virtual receptionist pricing](/blog/virtual-receptionist-pricing) is the breakdown.

### 3. An AI receptionist

A voice AI answers the line, holds a conversation, answers from information you gave it, takes the job details, and passes the lead to you.

**Good at:** the first ring, at 3am, on Christmas, on a flat monthly cost that does not punish a storm week. It does not have an off night.
**Bad at:** judgement, if it is built to always produce an answer. A caller with a burning smell, a complaint, or a situation you never documented needs a person. A confident wrong answer costs more than voicemail.

Most AI answering products do not book a calendar, take a card, or type into Jobber, Housecall Pro or ServiceTitan while the caller is still on the line. If those three things are the job, you are buying a live service. If the job is “answer the 10pm call, ask the four questions, and get it to me,” AI is usually the cheaper tool. The category write-up is in [AI receptionist for small business](/blog/ai-receptionist-for-small-business).

:::callout
**The question to ask any AI answering service.**
What does it do when it does not know the answer? If the honest reply is anything other than “it stops and hands off to a human,” keep looking. Improvising on an electrical call is how you turn a recoverable lead into a one-star review and, in the worst case, a safety problem.
:::

**Hiring instead**

The [Bureau of Labor Statistics *Occupational Outlook Handbook*](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/construction-and-extraction/electricians.htm) puts the median electrician wage at **$62,350 a year, $29.98 an hour**, from May 2024 data. That is one licensed person, on one shift. It does not cover lunch, sick days, or the Saturday the service drop comes down. Hiring is the right move if you also need a body at a desk. It is the wrong move if the job is “answer the 10pm call.”

Two traps.

**“Unlimited” with an asterisk.** If a plan says unlimited anything, find the fair-use clause. There is always one, and you will find it in the month a storm hits your service area.

**Per-minute maths that only work when you are quiet.** A per-minute plan looks cheap on a slow month and expensive on a good one. Your answering bill rises exactly when you are busiest and have least time to audit it. Model a busy month.

## What to ask before you sign

Six questions, in order of how much money they will save you.

1. **“Who answers at 11pm on a Sunday, and are they the same team as at 10am Tuesday?”** For a lot of providers the honest answer is no. That is the call you are buying.
2. **“Walk me through a burning-smell call.”** You want the questions asked, the threshold for “truck now,” and how your on-call electrician is reached. If it is vague in the sales call, it will be vague at 10pm too.
3. **“How do you bill a wrong number, a robocall, or a hang-up?”** On per-minute and per-call plans this is real money. Get it in the contract.
4. **“Will the person answering give electrical advice?”** They should not. Triage is “smoke or not, whole house or one circuit, address.” Diagnosis is your job. If the demo includes the operator telling a caller to reset a breaker, you have seen the failure mode.
5. **“What happens to the details after the call?”** A message you have to re-key is a message you will lose. Ask what you receive and where it lands — text, email, app, recording.
6. **“What’s the cancellation term?”** Annual lock-ins are common in this category. Smith.ai’s live page said month-to-month. Ask everyone else the same question and get it in writing.

Do not skip the Sunday-night question. An electrician answering service that is excellent at 10am and outsourced at 11pm is two different products sold as one.

## The four questions your answering service should ask every caller

Whatever you buy, the script matters more than the brand. For an electrical shop, four answers decide the night:

:::steps
Smoke, burning smell, or sparking? :: That is the truck-out-now question. If yes, everything else is secondary — get the address and get you on the phone.
Whole house or one circuit? :: A single dead outlet at 9pm is often a morning job. A dark house, especially with medical equipment in it, is not.
Anyone who cannot sit in the dark? :: Oxygen concentrators, elderly callers, small children. This changes whether you roll now or book tomorrow.
Address, and is it a rental? :: Travel distance decides whether the job is worth taking tonight. If a landlord has to approve the spend, the job is not booked yet no matter how urgent it sounds.
:::

An answering service that collects a name and a number has handed you a message. One that collects those four answers has handed you a job you can price before you get in the van.

Tell them, in the script, not to open a panel, not to reset a breaker if there is heat or a smell, and not to guess. The homeowner called an electrician. They can wait two minutes for you to look at the notes.

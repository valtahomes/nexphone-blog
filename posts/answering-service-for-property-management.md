It is 2:11am and a tenant in unit 4B is standing in the dark with water coming through the ceiling. She calls the office number — the only number she has — and it rings out to a mailbox that says the office reopens at nine.

By morning it is not a burst supply line any more. It is a burst supply line, ruined drywall in two units, an insurance claim, a furious tenant, and a habitability question you now have to answer in writing.

The call itself took forty seconds to answer. Everything that came after it did not.

The job is not answering. It is triage.
Any service can pick up a phone and take a message. What separates a useful property-management answering service from an expensive voicemail is whether the person on the line can tell a flooded unit from a dripping faucet — and knows exactly who to wake up for which.

## The short answer

**What it costs:** roughly **$50–$300 a month** on per-minute live plans (more in a bad month), **$2–$5 per call** on per-call plans, or **$20–$200 a month flat** for AI answering that does not bill by volume. Price the month your boiler dies, not the average.

**What you're choosing between:** a live call centre that takes messages and follows an escalation script, a virtual receptionist team that does more but costs considerably more, or an AI receptionist that answers instantly at any hour and passes the call on. They fail differently.

**What to judge it on:** the triage rules and the escalation path. Not the greeting, not the hold music, and not the sales demo — the written definition of what counts as an emergency and what happens next.

## Why property management punishes a missed call twice

Most businesses lose a missed call and lose a sale. Property management loses a missed call and takes on a liability. That is a different kind of problem, and it is why this category exists at all.

**Emergency maintenance has a legal clock.** Habitability obligations vary by state, but the pattern is consistent: no heat, no water, no working plumbing, or an unsafe condition triggers a duty to respond within a defined window. "Nobody picked up" is not a defence, and the paper trail of when a tenant reported a problem tends to matter later.

**A missed leasing call is rent you never collect.** A prospect calling about a vacant unit is on a list of five listings. If nobody answers, they tour someone else's building. Every day of vacancy has a price, and it is usually far more than a month of answering service.

**Small problems become large ones on a timer.** A slow leak reported Friday night and actioned Monday morning is not the same repair. Water damage compounds. So does mould, and so does tenant patience.

**And the calls are relentless and mostly not urgent.** Package questions, lockouts, noise complaints, rent questions, vendors, owners. The volume is what makes staffing it properly impossible for a small team — and the noise is what makes the genuinely urgent call easy to miss.

## The three kinds you can buy

### 1. A traditional live answering service (a call centre)

Operators answer as your management company, follow the script you supply, take a message, and escalate if your rule says to.

**Good at:** volume, sounding human, following an escalation rule you wrote carefully.
**Bad at:** knowing your properties. The operator does not know that 4B is above the parking garage, or that the boiler at the Elm Street building has failed twice this winter. They read the script. Anything the script did not anticipate becomes "someone will get back to you."

Watch the billing. Per-minute plans bill hold time, wrong numbers, robocalls and the four-minute call from a vendor. Ask specifically how non-tenant calls are billed before you sign.

### 2. A virtual receptionist

A smaller, better-trained team working from a longer script with more authority. Some will handle leasing inquiries properly, check availability, and book showings.

**Good at:** the professional front-office impression, complex or sensitive calls, leasing conversations that need judgement.
**Bad at:** the price, and the hours. This is the most expensive option per call, and after-hours is frequently handed to a different overflow team — which is precisely the coverage you were buying. Ask directly who answers at 2am, and whether they have your escalation rules in front of them.

### 3. An AI receptionist

A voice AI answers the line, holds a real conversation, works from information you supplied, captures the details and passes the call on.

**Good at:** answering instantly, at 2am, on a holiday weekend, during a storm when forty tenants call at once and a human queue would collapse. Flat cost, so a bad month does not produce a bad invoice.
**Bad at:** judgement, when it is built badly. A distressed tenant, a legal threat, or a situation nobody scripted needs a person. An AI that always produces an answer is worse than one that admits it does not know.

:::callout
**The one question that separates good AI from dangerous AI.**
"What does it do when it doesn't know?" If the answer is anything other than "it stops, says a person will follow up, and escalates," walk away. In property management a confidently wrong answer about habitability, entry rights or a repair timeline is not an inconvenience — it is evidence.
:::

## What it costs

| Model | Typical shape | You pay more when | Watch for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Per minute** | Base fee + a per-minute rate on a block | Storm weeks, cold snaps, anything that spikes call volume | Hold time and wrong numbers billed as minutes; rounding up |
| **Per call** | Roughly $2–$5 per answered call | Portfolio grows, or a building has a bad month | What counts as a "call" — some bill hang-ups and robocalls |
| **Flat monthly** | Roughly $20–$200/mo | Rarely — that is the point | Minute caps and what overage costs |
| **In-house after-hours** | On-call wages or a rotation | Always, and it does not scale down | One person cannot cover 24/7/365; turnover is brutal on this role |

### A 200-unit example

Two hundred units generates a lot of ordinary noise and a handful of calls a year that genuinely matter. Say a normal month runs 120 calls and January runs 260 because the heat fails in two buildings.

On per-minute billing, January is the month you get a bill you did not model — exactly when your team is least able to audit it. On flat AI pricing, January costs what June costs. On an in-house rotation, January is the month someone quits.

The comparison that decides it is not service A versus service B. It is what the service costs against one avoidable escalation: a water event caught at 2am instead of 8am, or one vacancy filled a week sooner. Most managers know their own number for that instinctively.

### The costs that are not on the price page

Three line items decide whether a cheap plan stays cheap.

**Overage.** Every plan has a cliff. Find where it is and what a minute costs past it, then look at your
worst month rather than your average one.

**Setup and script changes.** Some providers charge to build or revise your call script. If your
emergency list or your vacancies change often — and they do — a per-change fee turns into a recurring
one.

**The re-keying tax.** If the message arrives as an email that someone on your team retypes into your
property software, that labour is part of the cost, and it is where details get dropped. Ask what
format you actually receive.

## Emergency triage is the whole product

Everything else is packaging. Before you evaluate a single provider, write down your own emergency definition, because you are going to hand it to whoever answers.

A workable one usually includes: no heat when it is below a threshold, no water, no working toilet in a single-bathroom unit, active flooding or a burst pipe, sewage backup, gas smell, fire or CO alarm, electrical sparking or a total power loss, a broken exterior door or lock that leaves a unit unsecured, and a lockout policy you have actually decided on.

Everything else — a dripping tap, a noisy neighbour, a package, an appliance that still works — is a Monday work order, and the person answering has to be willing to say so politely at 2am.

:::steps
Identify the property and unit :: Nothing can be dispatched or logged without it, and a distressed caller will not always volunteer it.
Run the emergency test :: Is it on your written list? That single question decides everything downstream.
Escalate or log :: A true emergency reaches the on-call tech now, by a route you have tested. Everything else becomes a dated work order.
Record it :: Time, caller, unit, what was reported, what was said. In this business the record is as valuable as the response.
:::

## The half everyone forgets: leasing calls

Every provider in this category sells you on emergencies, because emergencies are frightening. But the
calls that pay for the service are usually the boring ones during business hours.

A prospect ringing about a vacant unit is on a shortlist of five listings and is calling them in order.
They are not going to leave a voicemail and wait. If your line rings out at 2pm on a Wednesday because
the whole team is at a turnover, that unit sits empty another week — and a week of vacancy on a single
unit typically costs more than a month of answering service.

So when you evaluate a provider, test both paths, not just the scary one:

:::steps
Call as a tenant with an emergency :: Does the agent identify the property, run your triage list, and escalate the way you wrote it?
Call as a prospect about a listing :: Do they know what is vacant, what it rents for, and what happens next — or do they take a name and lose the lead?
:::

The second call is the one most services quietly fail, because leasing needs current information —
availability, rent, pet policy, when someone can tour — and most scripts are written once and never
updated. Ask how the answering side learns that a unit went off the market this morning. If the answer
involves emailing someone a revised script, assume it will be stale.

## What to ask before you sign

1. **"Who answers at 2am on a holiday weekend, and are they the same team as Tuesday at 10am?"** Often the honest answer is no. That is the shift you are buying.
2. **"Walk me through an emergency, step by step."** You want a specific escalation path with a threshold and a tested route to your on-call person. Vague now means vague at 2am.
3. **"How do you bill wrong numbers, robocalls and hang-ups?"** On per-minute and per-call plans this is real money.
4. **"Can the agent handle a leasing inquiry, or only maintenance?"** Leasing calls are revenue and they arrive on the same line.
5. **"What record do I get, and how fast?"** A message you have to re-key is a message that gets lost. You want the log to be usable evidence.
6. **"What happens when forty tenants call in the same hour?"** Storms and outages do not arrive politely spaced. Ask what the queue does.

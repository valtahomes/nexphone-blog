The vinyl guy has the roll on the table and a Sharpie in his hand. You are in a sign shop on a Thursday, about to put a phone number in six-inch digits on the rear doors of a truck you will still be driving in 2029, and he is asking the only question that matters this afternoon: local, or 800?

You have been giving customers your cell for years. You do not want that cell on a wrap. The one-pager on the counter offers two kinds of business number. One matches the city. The other starts with 1-800 and, according to the guy behind the counter, "looks more professional."

:::callout
**The number on the van is a marketing choice you live with for years.**
It is not a phone-system feature. Pick it for who you want calling you, and for whether they believe you can show up today.
:::

## The short answer

A **toll-free number for business** is a US number that starts with 800, 888, 877, 866, 855, 844, or 833. The original deal was that the caller did not pay long-distance. The business did. That deal still exists on paper. On a mobile phone with nationwide calling, the caller was not going to pay anyway.

**Get toll-free** if people will ring you from more than one area code, you run ads that cover a region, or you want a number that does not advertise which suburb you live in.

**Get a local number** if you are a one-city trade (plumber, HVAC, electrician, cleaner, landscaper) and the person calling is trying to work out whether you can be there this afternoon. A local area code is the fastest way to say yes.

**Most one-truck businesses should start with local.** Add toll-free later if you actually take out-of-area work. Both numbers can ring the same phone. Print one of them on the wrap.

The rest of this page is the part the sign shop does not tell you: what those prefixes do in 2026, what they cost in practice, how texting fits, and how to set the number up before anyone peels the backing off the vinyl.

## What a toll-free number is

Toll-free is a class of phone number, not a brand of phone service. The Federal Communications Commission treats these numbers as ones where the **called party** is billed for the inbound call. The prefixes in use are:

- **800** — the original, still the one people can say out loud
- **888, 877, 866, 855, 844, 833** — added as 800 filled up; they work the same way

There is no extra magic in 800 versus 833. A caller who grew up with 1-800-FLOWERS may trust 800 slightly more. For a small business that is a rounding error. Do not pay a premium for an 800 prefix unless you have a vanity word that only works on 800.

A **vanity number** is a toll-free number that spells something. Radio ads love them. Brokers sell them. They cost more than a random 833, sometimes a lot more, and you still need a human or a voice AI on the other end. A memorable number that goes to voicemail is a memorable way to lose the job.

Toll-free numbers are handed out through the national toll-free registry, administered by Somos. In practice you do not apply to Somos. You get the number from a business phone provider, and that provider is the one responsible for keeping it alive. Buy the number from the company that will actually ring your phone. A cheap 800 parked at a broker you found in a search ad is a number you may not be able to move later.

## The part that changed: nobody pays long distance anymore

The 800 number was invented for a country where a call from Cleveland to Columbus was an item on a phone bill. That country is gone.

Most mobile plans include nationwide calling. The homeowner standing in a wet kitchen does not look at your prefix and think about cents per minute. They look at it and think: is this a local company, a national call centre, or a robocall?

So the original reason to get a toll-free number for business ("customers can call me for free") is mostly a sentence from a 1990s brochure. What you are buying now is a **signal**:

- You serve more than one town
- You have a number that can survive a move
- You have a number that is not your personal cell
- You have a number someone can remember from a radio ad

Those are real reasons. They are not the reason on the sign-shop one-pager.

If you only roll trucks inside one metro, the signal a local area code sends is stronger. It says you are already here.

## When a toll-free number still earns its place

Toll-free is the right main number in a short list of situations. If you are on this list, get one. If you are stretching to get on this list, you want a local number.

**You take work from several area codes.** A contractor who covers two states, a mobile cleaner who does Airbnb turnovers across a whole metro, a parts line that dealers call from wherever they are. The caller should not have to guess which suburb you parked in this year.

**You run ads that ignore city limits.** Radio, billboards on an interstate, packaging, a Facebook campaign aimed at a region. A local number on a regional ad makes you look like you only serve one exit. Toll-free matches the size of the ad.

**You do not want your cell on anything that outlives a job.** Invoices, the Google listing, the wrap, the yard sign that stays in a lawn for a week. A business number — toll-free or local — is how you stop giving strangers the phone you use to text your kids.

**You might move the shop.** A local number is tied to a rate centre. You can keep it if you leave town, but it will keep advertising the old town. Toll-free does not. Companies that expect to grow out of a single city often want one number they can keep for a decade.

**You have a vanity word worth saying on the radio.** This is the rare case where the number *is* the ad. Budget for the broker, then budget for someone to answer it at 7pm when the spot runs.

Property managers with after-hours tenant lines, regional contractors, and anyone selling into a 50-mile radius sit on this list more often than a one-van plumber does. The plumber still might want a second, toll-free number for the out-of-area ads. That is a second number, not a replacement for the local one on the truck.

## When a local number wins

For the businesses this blog is usually written for, local wins. The person calling is trying to hire someone who can drive over.

**"Near me" is the actual search.** Someone looking for an [answering service for plumbers](/blog/answering-service-for-plumbers) is in the same boat as someone looking for a plumber. They want a company that serves this street, tonight. A 216 or a 480 or a 713 on the listing matches that hope. An 800 makes them pause. The pause is where they dial the next result.

**Caller ID on the way back.** You will call this person from the number they rang, or from whatever your phone system sends. Unknown 800 numbers get treated like warranty scams and student-loan robocalls. A local caller ID gets picked up more often, which matters when you are confirming a window or asking where the shut-off is. Ask any provider, before you buy, which number shows when you dial out.

**The Google listing and the van should agree.** If the profile says you are in town, the number should look like it. Mismatched signals (a city name, a map pin, and a nationwide prefix) make a careful customer wonder which one is the truth.

**Emergency trades sell proximity.** Burst pipe, no heat, sparking panel. The ticket is large and the patience is short. The local number is doing sales work before you say hello.

A local number still hides your personal cell, forwards, and takes a [missed-call text-back](/blog/missed-call-text-back) after you miss the ring. You do not give up "looking like a business" by skipping 800. You pick a different kind of business to look like: one that is already in the neighbourhood.

## You can run both

This is the option the one-pager buries. You are choosing what to print this month, and what to point at a phone.

A common setup that works:

- **Local number** on the van, the invoices, the Google Business Profile, the yard signs
- **Toll-free number** on regional ads, packaging, and any campaign that covers more than one area code
- **Both ring the same device**, so you are not carrying two phones

Print one of them in the six-inch digits. Two numbers on a wrap looks like you could not decide, and the caller will pick at random. Put the second number on the website and the ads it belongs to.

Ask, in writing, which caller ID goes out when you return a call. If the system always sends the 800, local leads will screen you. If you can send the number they originally dialled, keep it that way.

Extra numbers on a VoIP plan are usually cheap relative to a missed job. The expensive part is still the same: somebody has to answer.

## What it costs, without the brochure

Pricing in this category hides in three different places. Treat these as shapes. Plans move, and an undated dollar figure is wrong by next quarter.

**The digits.** On a modern business phone plan, a local or toll-free number is often included, or added for a small monthly fee. This is the line item people obsess over. It is the smallest one.

**The inbound call.** Because the business is the billed party on a toll-free call, older carriers charged per inbound minute. Some still do. On a bundled VoIP plan the inbound call is usually another minute in the pile. Read the plan. If toll-free inbound is metered separately, a busy month costs more, which is the month you can least afford a surprise.

**The vanity.** A random 833 is a commodity. A number that spells your trade is a broker product. Budget for it like a piece of advertising, because that is what it is. Then remember it does not answer itself.

**The answering.** A number that rings a cell in a crawlspace is a voicemail box with better branding. The monthly cost that matters is whatever you pay to have the line picked up, qualified, and sent to you as a lead. That may be you, a person you hire, a live answering service, or an [AI receptionist for small business](/blog/ai-receptionist-for-small-business). Price that, then pick local or toll-free on top of it.

Two traps show up in sales calls.

**A "free" 800 with a contract.** The number is the hook. The lock-in is the product. Month-to-month is the default you should expect in 2026. Annual terms need a reason other than the prefix.

**Buying the number somewhere other than the phone system.** If a broker holds the number and a separate company rings it, you have two vendors and one way for the number to get stuck. Get the number from the company that will carry the calls.

## Texting, forwarding, and the part that is not instant

People text businesses now. They text the number on the van. They text after a missed call. If the new number cannot send a text, you have built a one-way street.

A few rules that save a wasted week:

**Calls and voicemail can work the day the number is issued.** Outbound business texting in the US needs a one-time carrier registration. That takes a few business days. Local numbers and toll-free numbers take slightly different registration paths. The practical sentence is the same: do not print "text us" on the wrap the afternoon you order the line.

**Forwarding carries calls, not texts.** If you keep your old number and forward it to a new one, the voice calls follow. SMS does not. Customers who text the number on last year's invoices will hit the old phone, or hit nothing. If you want the published number to send and receive texts, it has to live on the system that does the texting: issued there, or ported there.

**Missed-call text-back needs sending rights.** The useful version of a missed ring is a text back in seconds, so the caller does not dial the next plumber. That feature only exists once the number is allowed to send SMS. Plan the registration before you need the Saturday emergency.

**A landline and a toll-free number are different objects.** Toll-free is not "a landline with an 800." It is a registered service on a phone system. You can point it at a mobile. That is the whole point for a one-person trade.

If the job is "stop missing the people who already called," read the [missed-call text-back](/blog/missed-call-text-back) guide after this one. The prefix on the number does not fix a line that rings out.

## How to get a toll-free number without painting yourself into a corner

Do this in order. The wrap is last.

:::steps
Decide what the number is for :: Main number for a regional business, second number for ads, or a vanity you will say on the radio. That choice sets local vs toll-free, and whether you need both.
Issue it on the phone system that will answer it :: Local or toll-free, from the same provider that will ring your cell, take voicemail, and handle texts after registration. Do not park the number with a stranger.
Point it at a phone you will actually hear :: Forwarding, an app, a desk phone, a ring group. Test it from a second phone. Stay on the line. Leave a voicemail. Send a text once registration clears.
Ask which caller ID goes out :: Have them send you a test call and a test text. Save what arrives. That is what your customers will see when you ring them back.
Then print :: Van, Google listing, invoices, yard signs. One number in the big type. The second number, if you have one, goes on the ads it belongs to.
:::

Porting an existing toll-free in is possible. It is also slower than issuing a new one. If the old 800 is already on trucks and paper, port it and live with the wait. If it is not printed anywhere that matters, issuing a new number is cleaner.

Keep the cell off every public surface. The business number is the one that gets missed, recorded, and handed to whoever is on call. The cell is yours.

## What to put on the van

One number. The one you want a stranger to dial with wet hands.

Match it to the Google listing. Match the listing to the city you actually serve. If you cover two area codes and you are proud of that, toll-free on the regional ads and local on the truck is a split you can explain. Two equal numbers on the rear doors is not a split you can explain.

Test before vinyl. Call it from the parking lot. Call it after 6pm. Have someone else call it. If you have already paid for missed-call text-back, miss a call on purpose and see what arrives.

A toll-free number for business is a good tool for a business that is actually toll-free-shaped: more than one area code, ads that travel, a number you want to keep when you move. For a one-city trade, a local number does the sales work the 800 used to get credit for.

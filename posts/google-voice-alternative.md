You are on a ladder, one hand on a gutter, the other holding a drill. Your phone buzzes in your back pocket. It is a number you do not recognise. By the time you climb down it has gone to voicemail, and the caller has already tried the next gutter company on the list.

That phone is probably running on a personal number, or a free Google Voice line you set up years ago to keep your cell private. It worked fine when the business was a side job. It gets less fine every month you grow.

This guide covers what you can switch to, what each option costs as of this week, and which one fixes the problem you have.

## The short answer

**If you only need a second number**, Google Voice is hard to beat on price. Its Basic plan is $0 and its paid individual tiers are $10 and $20 a month.

**If you want a shared inbox, business texting and a proper phone menu**, a business phone app such as Quo or Grasshopper is the usual next step. Expect roughly $14 to $35 per user per month depending on the plan.

**If your real problem is calls you cannot answer**, a new phone app will not fix it. You need something that picks up for you, either a live receptionist (hundreds of dollars a month) or an AI receptionist (anywhere from free to a few hundred dollars a month).

Every price below comes from the vendor's own page, fetched 6 October 2026. Prices move, so confirm on the vendor's page before you buy.

## What Google Voice does well

Google Voice is a good product that gets used for a job it was not mainly built for. Most "alternatives" articles spend three paragraphs pretending it is bad.

From Google's own Voice page (checked 6 October 2026):

- **Basic is $0.** One number for all your devices, unlimited domestic calling and texting, and an existing mobile number is required.
- **Starter is $10 a month.** It adds 24/7 Google Voice support, three-way calling, call transfers, call recording, desk phone support, and a promise that your number will not be reclaimed for inactivity.
- **Standard is $20 a month**, currently listed at half price for the first six months. It adds auto attendants and Gemini call notes.
- **Team plans** run $10, $20 and $30 per user per month (Starter, Standard, Premier) and need a separate Google Workspace subscription. Standard adds ring groups and call queuing. Premier adds automatic call recording, transcription and AI notes.

For a one-person shop that wants a number separate from a personal cell, that is a lot for the money. If that describes you and nothing is going wrong, stay put.

## Where owners outgrow it

People leave Google Voice for a short list of reasons.

**They need the number to ring someone else.** A second person covering the phones needs ring groups, and on the team plans those start at the Standard tier, on top of a Workspace subscription.

**They want a phone menu or call flow.** Auto attendants arrive at Standard.

**They want business texting that behaves like a business tool.** Texting is included, but the shared-inbox, quick-reply, auto-reply style of tool is what phone apps built their products around.

**They want calls answered.** Google's page lists no AI that holds a conversation with the caller. A menu that says "press 1 for service" is not that.

That last one is the real fork in the road, and it decides which of the options below makes sense.

## Option 1: a business phone app

These replace Google Voice with something designed for a business line. The call still rings your phone. The difference is the tooling around it.

**Quo (formerly OpenPhone)**

Per Quo's pricing page, checked 6 October 2026:

- Starter is $19 per user per month, or $15 per user billed annually
- Business is $33 per user per month, or $23 billed annually
- Scale is $47 per user per month, or $35 billed annually
- Every plan includes one new or ported number per user, unlimited calling and messaging to US and Canadian numbers (subject to a fair usage policy), voicemail transcripts, and an AI agent called Sona with 1,000 free credits, which Quo says covers 10 calls
- Business adds AI call summaries and transcripts for all calls, phone menus, group calling, call transfers, auto call recording, and HubSpot and Salesforce integrations
- More Sona credits are sold in tiers, from $25 a month for 4,000 credits (40 calls) up to $199 a month for 60,000 credits (600 calls)

Quo also tells you plainly that business texting needs carrier registration. It lists a $19.50 one-time review fee and $1.50 to $3 a month. For how that process works, see [our 10DLC registration guide](/blog/10dlc-registration). If you are weighing Quo in depth, [Quo pricing](/blog/quo-pricing) and [Quo alternatives](/blog/quo-alternatives) go further.

**Grasshopper**

Grasshopper's pricing page (checked 6 October 2026) lists plans starting at $14 a month and a 7-day free trial with no credit card. The fetched page does not show the plan-by-plan feature list, so check what the $14 tier includes before you assume it covers texting or a shared inbox.

**Nextiva**

Nextiva sells a full business phone platform plus an AI receptionist called XBert. Its page (checked 6 October 2026) describes call answering, appointment booking, lead capture and a 30-day money-back guarantee, and gives no price, only a "Get My Quote" button. If you are looking at it, [Nextiva pricing](/blog/nextiva-pricing) collects what is publicly known.

**Who this fits:** owners who still plan to answer most calls themselves and want a cleaner line, a shared inbox, and some automation. The weak spot is the same as before. If you are under a sink, the phone still rings out.

## Option 2: a live receptionist service

A person answers as your business and sends you the details. This is the traditional answer to missed calls and it costs real money.

**Ruby**

Per Ruby's pricing page (checked 6 October 2026), receptionist plans are priced by minutes a month:

- 50 minutes for $250 a month
- 100 minutes for $395 a month
- 200 minutes for $720 a month
- 500 minutes for $1,725 a month

Ruby says every plan includes 24/7 live answering, call handling and lead intake. It also lists scheduling and payment collection as options.

**Smith.ai (human-first)**

Per Smith.ai's pricing page (checked 6 October 2026), the human-first plans bill by the call:

- Starter is $300 a month for 30 calls, with $11.50 for each call over
- Basic is $810 a month for 90 calls, with $10.50 over
- Pro is $2,100 a month for 300 calls, with $8.50 over

Smith.ai lists a 30-day money-back guarantee, no long-term contract, and no charge for spam calls that pass caller ID. Its add-ons, such as booking appointments at $1.50 a call or accepting payments at $1, are priced separately. For the rest of the math, see [Smith.ai pricing](/blog/smith-ai-pricing).

**Who this fits:** owners with high-ticket jobs, complicated intake, or customers who expect a person. If a missed call costs you a $2,000 job, $300 a month is a reasonable insurance premium. If most of your calls are small repair quotes, the math gets harder fast.

## Option 3: an AI receptionist

A voice AI answers the call, holds a conversation, takes the details and sends you the lead. The price range is wide, because the category is new and vendors are still experimenting.

**Smith.ai (AI receptionist)**

Smith.ai's AI plans (checked 6 October 2026) start with a free tier of 25 calls a month, then $3.00 per call beyond that. Pro is $150 a month for 75 calls at $2.00 each. Enterprise is $500 a month for 300 calls. The company says a call can be handed to a live agent when the AI cannot handle it.

**Quo's Sona and Nextiva's XBert**

Both sit inside larger phone products, as described above. They suit owners who want one vendor for the phone and the answering.

**Who this fits:** owners who cannot answer during jobs and do not want to pay a person to wait by the phone. The honest question to ask every vendor is what the AI does when it does not know the answer. If the response is anything other than "it hands off to a human," keep looking. ## Moving your number without breaking it

Whichever route you pick, the number customers already know is the asset. A few rules save a lot of grief:

- **Do not cancel the old service first.** Cancelling Google Voice before the port finishes can strand a number your customers still have.
- **Ask who does the port.** Quo says its team will help move a number in for free. Smith.ai says you can port a business number at no charge. Others vary.
- **Forwarding is a fallback.** Forwarding carries calls but not texts. If you want the same number to text, it needs to be ported.
- **Decide on a toll-free or local number.** [Our toll-free number guide](/blog/toll-free-number-for-business) covers when each makes sense, and [how to get a business phone number](/blog/how-to-get-a-business-phone-number) walks through the setup.

## How to choose

:::steps
Count the calls you miss :: Write down how many calls hit voicemail in a typical week. If it is two or three, a phone app is enough. If it is fifteen, you need something that answers.
Price one lost job :: Take your average ticket and multiply it by the missed calls you think turned into work. That number is your budget ceiling for answering.
Decide who answers :: A person, an AI, or you. Each has a different monthly cost and a different failure mode.
Check the number plan :: Ask any vendor how they move your existing number, how long it takes, and whether texting is included.
Test one real call :: Call the demo line at an awkward hour with a messy question. Judge it on what it does when it is stuck.
:::

Owners usually fall into one of two groups. The first needs a cleaner line and a shared inbox, and Google Voice's paid tiers or a phone app will do it. The second needs the phone answered while their hands are full, and for that a phone app only solves half the problem.

## Where Google Voice still wins

Google Voice is cheap, steady, and tied to the Google account you already have. If you take a handful of calls a week, check voicemails by hand, and have no team, it costs between $0 and $20 a month and asks very little of you. Switching adds a bill, a setup, and a number port. That only pays off when the line is costing you work.

It is 2:17 on a Thursday. You are halfway through a job — on a roof, under a sink, in a client's hallway with both hands full — and the phone in the truck starts ringing. The person calling found you on Google five minutes ago. They have a job that needs doing this week. They will not leave a voicemail. They will not try you again in the morning. They will hang up on the fourth ring and dial the next name on the list.

An AI receptionist for small business exists so that call still becomes a job.

:::callout
**The product is not the voice. The product is what happens after the greeting.**
Anyone can demo a cheerful pickup. What you are buying is whether the caller gets a real answer from *your* information, whether the lead reaches your phone, and whether the thing stays quiet when it does not know.
:::

## The short answer

**What it is:** a voice AI on a real business phone line that answers 24/7, holds a conversation, works from information you uploaded about the business, takes the job details, and passes the lead to you. It is not a "press 1" menu. It is not a person in a call centre. It is not a full-time hire.

**What it costs:** small-business AI plans usually sit in a **flat monthly** shape — tens of dollars on the low end, low hundreds on the high end. Hybrid products that keep a live agent behind the AI cost more and often add a per-call rate on top. Live virtual receptionists are a different product and a different bill; Smith.ai's human-first Starter plan was **$300 a month for 30 calls** on 19 August 2026.

**What it does not do:** most of the ones built for owners like you will not book the job into a calendar, will not take a card, and will not type anything into Jobber, Housecall Pro or ServiceTitan while the caller is still talking. A few of the more expensive tools will push a calendar slot. Almost none will run your field-service software for you. If those three things are the job, buy a live service.

**What to judge it on:** not the demo greeting. What it does when the caller asks something it was not told. That is the failure mode that turns a recoverable call into a one-star review.

## What an AI receptionist actually is

Strip the marketing and the job is narrow.

A customer dials the number on your van, your Google listing, your invoice. Something answers on the first or second ring, in your business's name, at 2pm or 2am. It asks the questions you told it to ask. It answers from a packet of information you gave it — hours, service area, what you do, what you do not do, how you handle emergencies — not from a search of the open internet. It writes down the job. It sends that job to you.

That is the whole product. Everything else is a vendor adding a feature, or claiming one.

It is worth being precise about what "AI" is doing here, because the category name is doing a lot of work. The useful part is not that the voice sounds human. The useful part is that the thing can hold a back-and-forth — "is the water still running?", "is this a rental?", "are you inside our service area?" — without a phone tree and without a person sitting in a headset farm. The unuseful part is any system that would rather invent an answer than admit it does not know.

:::callout
**The one question that sorts the category.**
"What does it do when it doesn't know the answer?" If the honest reply is anything other than "it stops and hands off to a person," keep walking. Improvising on your behalf is how you lose the customer *and* the review.
:::

## What happens on a real call

A competent setup looks like this. If a vendor cannot walk you through these steps with your own business as the example, you are listening to a demo, not a product.

:::steps
The line rings :: The caller dials your existing business number, or a new local or toll-free number you put on the van. Something picks up in a ring or two. No menu.
It talks, using your information :: Hours, service area, what you handle, what you do not. Not a web search. Not a guess. If you did not upload it, the AI should not say it.
It qualifies the job :: Name, number, address, what is broken, whether it is urgent. For a plumber that means water running, shut-off, rental. For an HVAC tech it means no cooling, age of the system, can they wait until morning.
It sends you the lead :: A text with the details, plus a recording, a transcript and a short summary you can read between jobs.
It stops when it should :: Complaint, edge case, anything outside the script — the call goes to a person, or to you, and the AI says so.
:::

Two extra pieces show up on most serious products and are worth having, even though they are not the reason you bought it.

**Missed-call text-back.** If a call is not answered — you declined it, the AI was off, the line was busy — the caller gets a text in seconds asking how you can help. That is a different feature from the voice AI, and it saves a different kind of job: the one where the phone came out of your pocket too late.

**A real US number, with the rest of a phone system behind it.** Menus for when you want them, call forwarding, a team inbox if more than one of you share the line, recordings you can search later. An AI bolted onto a personal cell is a gadget. An AI on a business line is a front desk.

## The three jobs it still cannot do

This is the part most comparison pages skip, because it shrinks the addressable market. It is also the part that stops you buying the wrong thing.

**It does not book the calendar — not reliably, and often not at all.** A handful of vendors will push an available slot into Calendly or a similar tool. Nextiva's XBert page (fetched 19 August 2026) leads with appointment booking. Smith.ai lists AI scheduling on its AI Receptionist plans, same date. That is a real feature on those products. It is not a universal feature of the category, and it is not the same as a dispatcher putting a job on a tech's day. If your definition of "answered" is "the Friday 2pm slot is now taken," confirm the calendar connection on a live call before you sign. Do not take the sales page's word.

**It does not take a payment.** Deposits, trip fees, card-on-file — that is still a live receptionist or a link you send afterwards. Smith.ai's human-first plans sell payment collection as a **$1.00 per call** add-on (19 August 2026). Treat that as a signal: the live product can do it, the cheap AI product generally cannot.

**It does not drive your field-service software.** Jobber, Housecall Pro, ServiceTitan and the rest of that stack are where the job becomes a job. Almost no AI receptionist types into them while the caller is on the line. A live virtual receptionist sometimes will, and you will pay live-receptionist prices for it. If "the ticket has to exist in Jobber before they hang up" is the requirement, you are not shopping in this category.

There is a fourth limit that is about judgement rather than software. An angry customer, a complaint, a "your tech scratched my floor" call, anything that needs a person to read the room — that has to go to a person. An AI that always produces an answer is worse than voicemail. Voicemail at least does not invent a policy.

## Why small businesses are looking at this instead of an answering service

The old product in this slot was a live answering service. A room of operators, a script you emailed them, a bill that moved with minutes or with calls. That product still exists, and for some owners it is still the right buy. The [cost breakdown](/blog/answering-service-cost) is the place to price it. The reason the AI version is the thing people are actually typing into Google now is simpler than the vendors make it sound.

**The bill does not punish a good month.** Per-minute and per-call plans get more expensive exactly when you are busiest and have the least time to audit the invoice. A flat AI plan does the opposite: a busy week costs the same as a quiet one. That matches how a one-truck business actually feels about the phone.

**After-hours is the same product as business hours.** A lot of live services answer your line with the A-team at 10am on Tuesday and a cheaper overflow shop at 11pm on Saturday. For a [plumber](/blog/answering-service-for-plumbers), an HVAC tech, an electrician, a locksmith — the 11pm call is the expensive one. Paying for coverage and then getting a different, worse service at the hour you bought it for is how this category burns people.

**The operator has never been under a sink.** A live answering service is good at sounding human and bad at knowing your trade. They will read the script. If the caller goes off-script, you get "someone will call you back." An AI working from *your* information is not smarter than a person. It is more consistent, and it does not get tired, and it does not decide your emergency is somebody else's problem at the end of a shift.

None of that means live is dead. Live still wins at judgement, at booking, at taking a card, and at anything that has to land in software during the call. The mistake is buying live prices for an after-hours message-taking job, or buying an AI and expecting it to be a dispatcher.

## The three things you will be sold

Every vendor in this search result is selling one of three products. They all use the words "AI receptionist." They are not the same.

### 1. A pure AI receptionist

A voice AI answers, qualifies, and passes you the lead. Flat monthly cost, or a cheap base plus a minutes cap. No person in the loop unless you are the person.

**Good at:** first-ring pickup, 3am, Christmas Day, a bill that does not move when volume does.
**Bad at:** anything that needs judgement or a booking. The quality of the call is the quality of the information you gave it. Garbage in, a polite wrong answer out.

This is the right shape for a solo owner or a small team whose actual problem is "I miss jobs because I cannot pick up."

### 2. A hybrid: AI first, live agent behind it

The AI takes the call. If the conversation goes sideways, a human receptionist takes over. Smith.ai's AI Receptionist is built this way — their own pricing page (19 August 2026) describes AI first with a transfer to a North America-based receptionist when something complex comes up.

**Good at:** the safety net. Callers who need a person get one without you being that person.
**Bad at:** the price, which starts to look like a live service once you add the per-call rate, and the fact that you now have two systems to configure. You are paying for the handoff. Make sure the handoff is real, not a voicemail with extra steps.

### 3. A live virtual receptionist with AI in the headline

A human answers as your front desk. They may use AI tools in the background — summaries, CRM updates, suggested replies. The caller is talking to a person. Smith.ai's human-first plans, Ruby, and the rest of the virtual-receptionist market live here.

**Good at:** booking, payments, complex handling, the polished "you reached an office" feeling.
**Bad at:** cost per call, and the after-hours question. Ask who answers at 2am. On a lot of these plans the honest answer is a different, cheaper team — or the AI you thought you were not buying.

## What an AI receptionist costs

Pricing in this category is easier than live answering, because most of it is a monthly number rather than a formula. It is still easy to compare the wrong things. These are the shapes, from vendor pages and the usual live-service maths, as of 19 August 2026. Treat them as shapes. Prices move.

**Flat monthly, AI only**

- Shape: a set fee that does not care how many times the phone rang, sometimes with a minutes cap
- Typical range: about **$20 to $200 a month** for a small-business product
- You pay more when: you blow a minutes cap and the overage is ugly, or you add a higher AI tier
- Watch: what "unlimited" actually means. There is always a fair-use clause. Find it before the month you need it.

**Base fee plus per-call, AI**

- Shape: a monthly platform fee, then a rate for each real call the AI handles
- Example, dated: Smith.ai AI Receptionist **Free** is $0 a month with 25 calls, then **$3.00 a call**. **Pro** is listed at **$150 a month** and **$2.00 a call**, with a 75-call tier. **Enterprise** starts at **$500 a month** and **$1.67 a call** (Smith.ai AI Receptionist pricing, 19 August 2026).
- You pay more when: volume climbs — which is the same trap as a live per-call plan, just with a nicer voice
- Watch: whether spam and wrong numbers count. Smith.ai says known spam is filtered and AI customers get an allowance to remove up to 10% of calls per cycle. Ask every other vendor the same question and get the answer in writing.

**Live virtual receptionist (not AI, sold next to it)**

- Shape: a bundle of calls per month, then overage
- Example, dated: Smith.ai human-first **Starter** is **$300 a month for 30 calls**, overage **$11.50 a call**. **Basic** is **$810 for 90**. **Pro** is **$2,100 for 300** (Smith.ai virtual receptionist pricing, 19 August 2026). Add-ons are extra: appointment booking **$1.50 a call**, payments **$1.00 a call**.
- You pay more when: you thought you were buying AI prices and signed up for a live plan
- Watch: the after-hours team. The 10am receptionist and the 11pm receptionist are not always the same people.

**Add-on to a phone system**

- Shape: a monthly AI fee on top of a per-user phone plan
- Example, dated: Nextiva XBert is **$99 a month for 100 interactions**, then **$0.99** each, on top of Nextiva Core at **$15 per user per month** (XBert product page and Nextiva pricing, 19 August 2026). An interaction is a call of 30 seconds or more, or a text/chat with at least three AI replies.
- You pay more when: you were comparing $99 to a standalone AI plan and skipped the phone-plan line
- Watch: you are buying Nextiva, not a $99 answering service

**Hiring instead**

A person at a desk is a salary, a shift, lunch, sick days, and a Saturday they are not working. It is the right buy if you also need a body in the office. It is the wrong buy if the job is "answer the phone at 11pm." An AI receptionist is not a hire. Do not compare it to one unless you were actually about to post the job.

Two traps, same as the live side of this market.

**A cheap headline with a minutes cap you will hit.** Model your actual inbound volume, including the wrong numbers, the parts-supplier calls, and the Saturday spike. If the plan goes quiet or dumps to voicemail when the AI minutes run out, that is the moment you bought it for.

**Comparing a $20 AI plan to a $300 live plan as if they do the same work.** They do not. The live plan can book and take a card. The AI plan answers and texts you. Price them against the job you need done, not against each other.

## What to test before you buy

Ignore the branded demo. Use your number, your script, your worst call.

1. **"Call us at 11pm on a Sunday and tell me what happens."** Then do it. You want to hear the pickup, the questions, and the text landing on your phone. A recording of a happy path at 2pm on a Wednesday is not a test.
2. **"What does it say when I ask something you were not told?"** Ask about a service you do not offer, or a suburb you do not cover, or a complaint. The correct behaviour is a clean stop and a handoff. The incorrect behaviour is a confident yes.
3. **"Show me the lead I receive."** A name and a number is a message. A name, a number, an address, the problem, and whether it is urgent is a job you can price before you get in the van.
4. **"Does texting work on day one?"** On a real US business line, outbound business texting usually needs a one-time carrier registration that takes a few business days. Calls and voicemail should work immediately. A vendor who says texts fly the same afternoon is skipping a step the carriers do not let them skip.
5. **"Can I keep the number on the van?"** Forwarding keeps the number and carries the calls. It does not carry texts. If you want the same number to text customers, it has to be ported. Ask which path they actually support.
6. **"What is the cancellation term?"** Month-to-month is common in AI. Annual lock-ins are common in live. Smith.ai's AI Receptionist is month-to-month with 30 days' notice (their FAQ, 19 August 2026). Get the equivalent in writing from whoever you are about to pay.

If you are in a trade where the expensive call arrives after dark, run the same 11pm test the plumbing guide already linked above is built around. The tool changes. The test does not.

## Who should not buy one

An AI receptionist is a bad purchase for a short list of owners, and it is cheaper to be on that list now than after a month of frustrated callers.

**You need the job on the calendar before they hang up.** Buy a live virtual receptionist, or one of the AI products that actually connect to a calendar, and watch a real booking happen on a recorded call before you switch the Google number over.

**You need a deposit taken on the call.** That is a live service. Budget for it.

**You need the ticket to appear in Jobber, Housecall Pro or ServiceTitan during the conversation.** Also a live service, and not all of those will do it either. Ask for a screen recording, not a promise.

**You wanted a person at a desk.** Hire. The AI will not greet walk-ins, will not sign for a delivery, and will not be a second pair of hands.

**Your volume is a contact centre's volume.** If you are taking hundreds of calls a day across a team, you are shopping for a phone platform with an AI layer — Nextiva and its peers — not a small-business receptionist. Different product, different buyer.

Everyone else on this page is a one-person or small-team business whose phone is the front door, and who loses a job every time that door is a voicemail. That is the buyer the category was built for.

It is 2:17 on a Thursday. You are halfway through a job (on a roof, under a sink, in a client's
hallway with both hands full) and the phone in the truck starts ringing. The person calling found
you on Google five minutes ago. They have a job this week. They will hang up on the fourth ring
and dial the next name on the list.

An **AI receptionist for small business** is how that call still becomes a job.

:::callout
**Buy the handoff, not the greeting.**
Anyone can demo a cheerful pickup. You want a real answer from *your* information, the lead on
your phone, and silence when the thing does not know.
:::

## The short answer

A voice AI on a real business phone line that answers 24/7, talks from information you uploaded,
takes the job details, and passes the lead to you. No "press 1" menu. No headset farm. No
full-time hire.

Small-business AI plans are usually a **flat monthly** fee: tens of dollars on the low end, low
hundreds on the high end. Hybrid products that keep a live agent behind the AI cost more and
often add a per-call rate. Live virtual receptionists are a different bill. Smith.ai's
human-first Starter plan was **$300 a month for 30 calls** on 19 August 2026.

Most of these tools will not book the job into a calendar, take a card, or type into Jobber,
Housecall Pro or ServiceTitan while the caller is still talking. A few of the more expensive
ones will push a calendar slot. Almost none will run your field-service software. If those are
the job, buy live.

Judge it on what it does when the caller asks something it was not told. That is the failure
that turns a recoverable call into a one-star review.

## What it is

A customer dials the number on your van, your Google listing, your invoice. Something answers
on the first or second ring, in your business's name, at 2pm or 2am. It asks the questions you
told it to ask. It answers from a packet you gave it (hours, service area, what you do, what
you do not, how you handle emergencies), not from a search of the open internet. It writes down
the job and sends that job to you.

The useful part is the back-and-forth: "is the water still running?", "is this a rental?", "are
you inside our service area?" without a phone tree and without a person in a headset farm. The
failure is any system that would rather invent an answer than admit it does not know.

:::callout
**Ask this before you sign.**
"What does it do when it doesn't know the answer?" If the reply is anything other than "it
stops and hands off to a person," keep looking. Improvising on your behalf loses the customer
and the review.
:::

## What happens on a real call

If a vendor cannot walk you through these steps with your own business as the example, you are
listening to a demo.

:::steps
The line rings :: The caller dials your existing business number, or a new local or toll-free number you put on the van. Something picks up in a ring or two. No menu.
It talks from your information :: Hours, service area, what you handle. Not a web search. If you did not upload it, the AI should not say it.
It qualifies the job :: Name, number, address, what is broken, whether it is urgent. For a plumber: water running, shut-off, rental. For HVAC: no cooling, age of the system, can they wait until morning.
It sends you the lead :: A text with the details, plus a recording, a transcript and a short summary you can read between jobs.
It stops when it should :: Complaint, edge case, anything outside the script. The call goes to a person, or to you, and the AI says so.
:::

Two extras show up on most serious products.

**Missed-call text-back.** If a call is not answered (you declined it, the AI was off, the line
was busy) the caller gets a text in seconds asking how you can help. That is a different
feature from the voice, and it saves the job where the phone came out of your pocket too late.

**A real US number, with a phone system behind it.** Menus when you want them, call forwarding,
a team inbox if more than one of you share the line, recordings you can search later. An AI
bolted onto a personal cell is a gadget. An AI on a business line is a front desk.

## What it still cannot do

**Calendar booking is not the default.** Nextiva's XBert page (fetched 19 August 2026) leads
with appointment booking. Smith.ai lists AI scheduling on its AI Receptionist plans, same
date. That is a real feature on those two. It is not the category, and it is not a dispatcher
putting a job on a tech's day. If "answered" means "the Friday 2pm slot is taken," confirm the
calendar connection on a live call. Do not take the sales page's word.

**It does not take a payment.** Deposits, trip fees, card-on-file still belong to a live
receptionist or a link you send afterwards. Smith.ai's human-first plans sell payment
collection as a **$1.00 per call** add-on (19 August 2026). Treat that as a signal: the live
product can do it, the cheap AI product generally cannot.

**It does not drive Jobber, Housecall Pro or ServiceTitan.** Those apps are where the job
becomes a job. Almost no AI receptionist types into them while the caller is on the line. A
live virtual receptionist sometimes will, and you will pay live-receptionist prices for it. If
the ticket has to exist in Jobber before they hang up, you are not shopping in this category.

Judgement is a fourth limit. An angry customer, a "your tech scratched my floor" call, anything
that needs a person to read the room has to go to a person. An AI that always produces an
answer is worse than voicemail. Voicemail at least does not invent a policy.

## Why people type this instead of "answering service"

The old product in this slot was a live answering service: a room of operators, a script you
emailed them, a bill that moved with minutes or with calls. That product still exists. The
[cost breakdown](/blog/answering-service-cost) is the place to price it. Search has moved
because three things about the live version are a poor fit for a one-truck phone.

**The bill does not punish a good month.** Per-minute and per-call plans get more expensive
exactly when you are busiest and have the least time to audit the invoice. A flat AI plan does
the opposite: a busy week costs the same as a quiet one.

**After-hours is the same product as Tuesday at 10am.** A lot of live services put the A-team
on the line at 10am and a cheaper overflow shop at 11pm on Saturday. For a
[plumber](/blog/answering-service-for-plumbers), an HVAC tech, an electrician, the 11pm call is
the expensive one. Paying for coverage and then getting a worse service at the hour you bought
it for is how this category burns people.

**The operator has never been under a sink.** A live answering service is good at sounding
human and bad at knowing your trade. They will read the script. If the caller goes off-script,
you get "someone will call you back." An AI working from *your* information is not smarter than
a person. It is more consistent, it does not get tired, and it does not decide your emergency
is somebody else's problem at the end of a shift.

Live still wins at judgement, booking, taking a card, and anything that has to land in software
during the call. The mistake is paying live prices for after-hours message-taking, or buying an
AI and expecting a dispatcher.

## Three products hiding under one name

Every vendor in this search result uses the words "AI receptionist." They are selling different
things.

### 1. Pure AI

A voice answers, qualifies, and passes you the lead. Flat monthly cost, or a cheap base plus a
minutes cap. No person in the loop unless you are the person.

First-ring pickup, 3am, Christmas Day, a bill that does not move when volume does. Weak on
judgement and booking. The quality of the call is the quality of the information you gave it.

Right shape for a solo owner or a small team whose problem is "I miss jobs because I cannot
pick up."

### 2. AI first, live agent behind it

The AI takes the call. If the conversation goes sideways, a human receptionist takes over.
Smith.ai's AI Receptionist is built this way. Their pricing page (19 August 2026) describes AI
first with a transfer to a North America-based receptionist when something complex comes up.

Callers who need a person get one without you being that person. The price starts to look like
a live service once you add the per-call rate, and you now have two systems to configure. You
are paying for the handoff. Make sure the handoff is a person, not voicemail with extra steps.

### 3. A live receptionist with AI in the headline

A human answers as your front desk. They may use AI in the background for summaries, CRM
updates, suggested replies. The caller is talking to a person. Smith.ai's human-first plans,
Ruby, and the rest of the virtual-receptionist market live here.

Booking, payments, complex handling, the "you reached an office" feeling. Cost per call is
high, and after-hours is the question. Ask who answers at 2am. On a lot of these plans the
honest answer is a different, cheaper team, or the AI you thought you were not buying.

## What it costs

Most of this category is a monthly number rather than a formula. You can still compare the
wrong things. Shapes, from vendor pages, 19 August 2026. Prices move.

**Flat monthly, AI only**

- A set fee that does not care how many times the phone rang, sometimes with a minutes cap
- About **$20 to $200 a month** for a small-business product
- The bill jumps when you blow a minutes cap, or you add a higher AI tier
- "Unlimited" always has a fair-use clause. Find it before the month you need it.

**Base fee plus per-call, AI**

- A monthly platform fee, then a rate for each real call the AI handles
- Smith.ai AI Receptionist **Free**: $0 a month with 25 calls, then **$3.00 a call**. **Pro**:
  **$150 a month** and **$2.00 a call**, 75-call tier. **Enterprise**: **$500 a month** and
  **$1.67 a call** (Smith.ai AI Receptionist pricing, 19 August 2026)
- Volume climbs, the bill climbs, same trap as a live per-call plan
- Ask whether spam and wrong numbers count. Smith.ai says known spam is filtered and AI
  customers can remove up to 10% of calls per cycle. Get the equivalent in writing from
  everyone else.

**Live virtual receptionist (sold next to the AI)**

- A bundle of calls per month, then overage
- Smith.ai human-first **Starter**: **$300 a month for 30 calls**, overage **$11.50 a call**.
  **Basic**: **$810 for 90**. **Pro**: **$2,100 for 300** (Smith.ai virtual receptionist
  pricing, 19 August 2026). Add-ons: appointment booking **$1.50 a call**, payments
  **$1.00 a call**
- Watch the after-hours team. The 10am receptionist and the 11pm receptionist are not always
  the same people.

**Add-on to a phone system**

- A monthly AI fee on top of a per-user phone plan
- Nextiva XBert: **$99 a month for 100 interactions**, then **$0.99** each, on top of Nextiva
  Core at **$15 per user per month** (XBert product page and Nextiva pricing, 19 August 2026).
  An interaction is a call of 30 seconds or more, or a text/chat with at least three AI replies
- You are buying Nextiva, not a $99 answering service

**Hiring instead**

A person at a desk is a salary, a shift, lunch, sick days, and a Saturday they are not
working. Hire them if you also need a body in the office. Do not hire them to answer the phone
at 11pm.

Two traps.

**A cheap headline with a minutes cap you will hit.** Model inbound volume including wrong
numbers, parts-supplier calls, and the Saturday spike. If the plan dumps to voicemail when the
AI minutes run out, that is the moment you bought it for.

**Comparing a $20 AI plan to a $300 live plan as if they do the same work.** The live plan can
book and take a card. The AI plan answers and texts you. Price them against the job you need
done.

## What to test before you buy

Ignore the branded demo. Use your number, your script, your worst call.

1. **"Call us at 11pm on a Sunday and tell me what happens."** Then do it. You want the pickup,
   the questions, and the text landing on your phone. A recording of a happy path at 2pm on a
   Wednesday is not a test.
2. **"What does it say when I ask something you were not told?"** Ask about a service you do
   not offer, a suburb you do not cover, or a complaint. Correct: a clean stop and a handoff.
   Incorrect: a confident yes.
3. **"Show me the lead I receive."** A name and a number is a message. A name, a number, an
   address, the problem, and whether it is urgent is a job you can price before you get in the
   van.
4. **"Does texting work on day one?"** Outbound business texting on a real US line usually
   needs a one-time carrier registration that takes a few business days. Calls and voicemail
   should work immediately. A vendor who says texts fly the same afternoon is skipping a step
   the carriers do not let them skip.
5. **"Can I keep the number on the van?"** Forwarding keeps the number and carries the calls.
   It does not carry texts. If you want the same number to text customers, it has to be
   ported.
6. **"What is the cancellation term?"** Month-to-month is common in AI. Annual lock-ins are
   common in live. Smith.ai's AI Receptionist is month-to-month with 30 days' notice (their
   FAQ, 19 August 2026). Get the equivalent in writing.

## Who should not buy one

**You need the job on the calendar before they hang up.** Buy a live virtual receptionist, or
one of the AI products that actually connect to a calendar, and watch a real booking on a
recorded call before you switch the Google number over.

**You need a deposit taken on the call.** That is a live service. Budget for it.

**You need the ticket in Jobber, Housecall Pro or ServiceTitan during the conversation.** Also
a live service, and not all of those will do it either. Ask for a screen recording, not a
promise.

**You wanted a person at a desk.** Hire. The AI will not greet walk-ins, will not sign for a
delivery, and will not be a second pair of hands.

**Your volume is a contact centre's volume.** Hundreds of calls a day across a team is a phone
platform with an AI layer (Nextiva and its peers), not a small-business receptionist.

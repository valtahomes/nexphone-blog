It is Sunday, 8:41pm. A one-van HVAC owner is at the kitchen table with three PDFs open.

One quote is $275 a month for 200 minutes. One is $9.75 a call with a 30-call minimum. One says "flat" $199 and, three paragraphs down, caps you at 75 calls. None of them say what a wrong number costs. None of them say who picks up at 11pm.

That pile of PDFs is the problem. **Answering service cost** is a billing shape, plus a definition of "a call," plus whatever the plan does when you are busy.

## The short answer

A live answering service for a small business typically costs **$135 to $400 a month** on a minute bundle. That range is from Ambs Call Center's 2026 pricing guide (published 6 July 2025). Nextiva's 13 July 2026 cost guide puts live plans in a wider band of **$100 to $1,000+** and AI answering at **$50 to $300 a month**.

The three shapes you will be quoted:

**Per minute.** You pay talk time, usually after a base fee. Typical 2026 band: **$0.75–$1.50 / min** (Nextiva, 13 July 2026). The bill jumps when calls run long, or when the vendor rounds every call up to a full minute.

**Per call.** One fee per answered call. Typical 2026 band: **$1–$11 / call** (Nextiva, same date); live starters often sit at the top of that. The bill jumps when volume climbs, or when hang-ups and wrong numbers count as calls.

**Flat / bundled.** A monthly fee for a stated envelope. Live bundles from about **$135** (Ambs). Nextiva puts AI answering at **$50–$300 a month**. The bill jumps when you blow the envelope, or when the "unlimited" fair-use clause appears.

Do not pick from the headline rate. Price a month of *your* calls, including the ones that were spam, on each shape. Then decide.

:::callout
**The bill is not the cost.**
The cost is the bill plus every job that still went to voicemail. A $250 plan that misses the after-hours emergency is more expensive than a $400 plan that catches it.
:::

## What the three pricing models charge you for

### Per-minute

You buy a block of minutes. Talk time burns the block. Overage is a published per-minute rate.

This looks fair. You pay for work done. The traps are in the increment and in what counts as talk time.

A vendor that rounds every call up to 60 seconds turns a 20-second "sorry, wrong number" into a billable minute. Hold time and transfer time are billable at some centres and not at others. A busy month of short calls can cost more than a quiet month of long ones, which is the opposite of what the sales page implied.

Per-minute is the right shape if your calls are short, your volume is lumpy, and you have the logs to audit the invoice.

### Per-call

You pay once per answered call, no matter how long it lasted.

This is easy to forecast if you know your call count. It is a bad deal on a line full of 40-second "are you open Saturday?" calls, and a good deal on a 12-minute emergency intake.

The question that matters is the definition. Some vendors bill a hang-up after two rings. Some bill a robocall. Smith.ai's live receptionist FAQ, checked 17 August 2026, says they do **not** count spam or wrong numbers against the quota if caller ID is passed through. That is not the industry default. Ask.

### Flat or bundled

You pay one monthly number for a stated envelope of minutes or calls. Stay inside it and the bill does not move. That is the point.

Two things to read before you believe the word "flat."

**The cap.** A $199 "flat" plan with a 75-call ceiling is a per-call plan with a bulk discount. Past the cap you are on overage, often at a worse rate than an honest per-call plan.

**The asterisk.** If the page says unlimited, find the fair-use clause. There is always one, and you will meet it in the month you actually needed the capacity.

## Answering service cost on one real-looking month

Industry pages love ranges. Ranges do not help you write a cheque. Here is the same month run through the three shapes, using numbers a one-truck trade actually sees.

**The model.** 100 inbound calls in a month. Average handle 2.5 minutes. That is 250 billable minutes if every call is answered and timed that way. Swap in your own last-month log.

Same 100 calls / 250 minutes, priced on pages we opened:

- **Live minute bundle, 250 minutes** — **$330–$525**. Source: Ambs' 250-minute band, 2026 guide.

Read that list twice. The same 100 calls cost anywhere from about $200 to more than a thousand. The cheap end is not "unlimited AI." It is a stated envelope. If an AI is going to answer most of a 250-minute month, the included block on an entry plan usually will not cover it — you are buying a larger tier, or you are buying overflow for the calls you miss, not a full-time front desk.

## What the invoice hides

These are the line items that make the invoice bigger than the quote.

**Rounding.** Ask whether they bill in 6-second, 30-second or 60-second increments. Full-minute rounding on a line of short calls inflates the invoice; we have not measured by how much across vendors. Ask, because this is the first place the quote and the invoice diverge.

**Hang-ups and wrong numbers.** On per-call plans this is real money. On per-minute plans it is smaller, unless they round.

**The 13th invoice.** Ambs' 2026 guide describes a four-week billing cycle that produces 13 invoices a year. A "$250 a month" plan is $3,250 a year, not $3,000. Ask how long a "month" is.

**After-hours and holidays.** Some live plans are priced on 9–5 weekdays and surcharge evenings, weekends and federal holidays. Those are the calls a one-truck owner is buying the service for. Ask who answers at 11pm on a Sunday, and whether they are the same team as Tuesday at 10am.

**Setup, scripts, "training."** Nextiva's July 2026 guide puts setup fees at $50–$500. Ruby and Smith both say no setup fee on the pages we checked. Others will not say until the contract.

**What a "call" is.** Texts, emails and faxes billed as calls still happen. Ambs flags it. Get the definition in the order form, not the brochure.

## Hiring a receptionist instead

The [Bureau of Labor Statistics *Occupational Outlook Handbook*](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/office-and-administrative-support/receptionists.htm) (last modified 28 August 2025) puts the median receptionist wage at **$17.90 an hour, $37,230 a year**, from May 2024 data. Most work full time. That is one person, on one shift, in one place.

It does not cover lunch, sick days, or the Saturday the pipe bursts. A second person to cover those hours is a second wage. Payroll tax and whatever you pay for benefits sit on top of the wage; we are not going to invent a burden rate.

Hire the person if you need a body at a desk: visitors, packages, a waiting room. Do not hire them to be a 24/7 phone tree. That is the job an answering service, or an AI on the line, is actually for.

## How to price your own month

Pull last month's call log from the phone you use now. Do this before you talk to a salesperson.

:::steps
Count the calls :: Inbound answered plus inbound missed. Missed still have to be answered by whoever you hire.
Time them :: Average handle, in minutes. If you do not have it, time the next 20 calls and use that.
Multiply :: Calls × minutes = the per-minute month. Calls alone = the per-call month.
Ask four questions :: What is a call? What is the increment? Who answers after 6pm? What is the overage rate, in writing?
Model a busy month :: Use your worst recent month, not the average. The bill rises when you can least sit down and read it.
:::

If the live quote is more than one emergency job, and the AI quote is less than one, the decision is not about the brand on the quote. It is about whether you will book the job yourself after the call.

It is 4:12 on a Saturday in August. The thermostat in a two-story house on the west side of town has been climbing since noon. It is 98 outside and 84 inside, a baby is crying in the next room, and the homeowner is calling every HVAC company on the first page of Google until somebody picks up.

They will not leave a voicemail. They will not wait until Monday. The shop that answers that call gets a same-day no-cool ticket and a customer who will call again the next time the coil ices over.

Any answering service can take a name during office hours. The question is what happens when the AC dies on a Saturday in a heat wave — whether the call gets triaged, whether it reaches you, and what a month of that coverage actually costs.

## The short answer

**What it costs:** a live HVAC answering service usually lands between **$250 and $800 a month** once a busy summer is priced honestly, roughly **$8–$12 a call** on a per-call live plan, or **$20–$200 a month flat** for an AI receptionist that does not bill by volume. Prices move. Treat those as shapes, and always model a July week, not a February average.

**What you are choosing between:** a live call centre that takes messages, a virtual receptionist working from a longer script, or an AI receptionist that answers instantly and passes the job to you. They fail in different ways. The right one depends on how hard your phone rings after 5pm in August, and what one missed no-cool call is worth.

**What to judge it on:** the escalation. What happens between the caller saying "there's no air coming out" and your phone buzzing.

## Why HVAC is a bad trade to miss calls in

Most businesses lose a missed call and lose a customer. HVAC loses a missed call in July and loses a same-day emergency customer, which is a different kind of money.

The [Bureau of Labor Statistics *Occupational Outlook Handbook*](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/installation-maintenance-and-repair/heating-air-conditioning-and-refrigeration-mechanics-and-installers.htm) (May 2024 data, last modified 28 August 2025) puts the median HVAC mechanic and installer wage at **$59,810 a year, $28.75 an hour**. That is one licensed person, on one shift. It does not cover the Saturday the condenser dies, and it does not cover the second phone that starts ringing while that person is on a roof.

Three things make the trade unusually punishing.

**The work and the phone fight for the same hands.** You are on a roof, in an attic, under a manufactured home, or standing in a closet with a manifold gauge set. The phone is in a pocket you cannot reach. The window in which the caller is still deciding who to hire is measured in minutes.

**Volume is seasonal, and the expensive calls bunch up.** February is quiet. The first 98-degree Saturday is not. A plan that looks cheap on a 40-call month will surprise you on a 160-call week, and that week is when a missed call costs the most.

**Callers in a hot house have no patience for a menu.** Somebody sitting in 84-degree air with a baby does not want to press 1 for service. They want a human sentence within a couple of rings. A recording sends them to the next result.

Any answering service worth paying for has to be good at the moment you are least able to answer. That is why the sales page matters less than the after-hours rules.

## The three kinds of answering service for an HVAC company

### 1. A traditional live answering service (a call centre)

A room of operators answers as your business, follows a script you supply, takes a message, and emails or texts it to you.

**Good at:** sounding human, handling a spike, following an escalation rule if you write one.
**Bad at:** knowing anything about HVAC. The operator has never charged a system. They read your script. If the caller says something the script did not anticipate — a tripped breaker that looks like a dead compressor, a homeowner who already "recharged" the unit with a can from the auto parts store — the answer is usually "someone will get back to you."

Watch the billing. Per-minute pricing means you pay for hold time, for the parts-house wrong numbers, for the warranty companies, and for the five-minute call that turned out to be a neighbour asking if you do mini-splits. Ask how they bill non-customer calls before you sign.

### 2. A virtual receptionist

The same idea, further upmarket: a smaller team, sometimes a named person, working from a longer script with more authority. Some will book jobs into a calendar and take a card over the phone.

**Good at:** the polished front-desk impression, complex handling, a caller who needs to be walked through a few extra questions.
**Bad at:** the price. This is the most expensive option per call by a wide margin, and it is still a human with working hours and an inbox. After-hours often means a different, cheaper overflow team answering your line at 9pm, which is precisely the call you were buying coverage for. Ask directly who answers at 2am, and ask again who answers during a heat-wave Sunday.

### 3. An AI receptionist

A voice AI answers your line, holds a conversation, answers from information you gave it, takes the job details, and passes the lead to you.

**Good at:** picking up on the first ring, at 3am, on the Saturday the heat index hits 110, for a flat monthly cost that does not punish a busy month. It does not have an off night and it does not quit in week two of a heat wave.
**Bad at:** judgement, if it is built badly. An AI that always produces an answer is worse than no AI. A caller with a complaint, a warranty dispute, or a carbon monoxide alarm needs a person, and a confident wrong answer costs more than a message would have.

Most AI answering products do **not** book a calendar, take a card, or type into ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro or Jobber while the caller is still on the line. If those three things are the job, you are buying a live service. If the job is "answer the 4pm Saturday call, qualify it, and get it to me," AI is usually the cheaper tool. ## What an HVAC answering service costs

Pricing in this category is hard to compare, because the three models hide cost in different places. The industry-wide breakdown lives in [what an answering service costs](/blog/answering-service-cost). The HVAC-specific version is below. Vendor figures in this section were checked on **25 August 2026**.

**Per minute (live)**

- Shape: a base fee plus included minutes, then overage
- A dated example: [Ruby](https://www.ruby.com/plans-and-pricing/) lists **$250/month for 50 minutes**, **$395 for 100**, **$720 for 200**, and **$1,725 for 500**, as of 25 August 2026. Same feature set on every tier. Optional extras include scheduling and payment collection.
- You pay more when: calls run long, or July arrives
- Watch: hold time, wrong numbers and spam billed as minutes; rounding up to the full minute; a heat-wave week that burns a 50-minute plan by Wednesday

**Per call (live)**

- Shape: a monthly bundle of answered calls, then a steep overage rate
- A dated example: [Smith.ai's live receptionist plans](https://smith.ai/pricing/receptionists) list **$300/month for 30 calls** ($11.50 overage), **$810 for 90** ($10.50 overage), and **$2,100 for 300** ($8.50 overage), as of 25 August 2026. Month-to-month. They state they do not charge for spam if caller ID is passed through. Booking a calendar is an extra **$1.50 per call**. Taking a payment is an extra **$1.00**.
- You pay more when: volume climbs, which is exactly August
- Watch: what counts as a "call," and whether after-hours is the same team

**Flat monthly or per-call AI**

- Shape: a low monthly base, sometimes with a per-call meter on top
- A dated example: [Smith.ai's AI receptionist](https://smith.ai/pricing/ai-receptionist) lists a free tier at **25 calls/month** then **$3.00 a call**, a Pro plan at **$150/month** and **$2.00 a call** (75 calls included), and Enterprise at **$500/month** and **$1.67 a call** (300 included), as of 25 August 2026. Other AI products charge a flat **$20–$200 a month** and stop metering.
- You pay more when: the plan has a call cap and July exceeds it
- Watch: whether spam counts, whether there is a minutes cap, and what happens when the cap is hit: voicemail, a live handoff, or a surprise bill

**Hiring instead**

That BLS median of **$59,810 a year** is one licensed tech, days only. A front-desk hire is cheaper per hour and still cannot cover 24/7. Hiring is the right move if you also need a body at a desk, someone to greet walk-ins and chase parts. It is the wrong move if the job is "answer the phone at 9pm when the AC is dead."

Two traps show up constantly in this trade.

**A winter price for a summer phone.** HVAC call volume is not a straight line. A per-minute or per-call plan that feels reasonable in March will spike in the first heat wave, which is also when you have the least time to audit the invoice. Model the worst week you actually had last July.

**"Unlimited" with an asterisk.** If a plan says unlimited anything, find the fair-use clause. There is always one, and you will find it in the week you need the capacity.

### A one-truck summer example

Say you take **120 inbound calls a month** in the cooling season, half of them after 5pm or on weekends, and a same-day no-cool ticket — trip, diagnosis, a capacitor or a charge, labour — often lands somewhere between **$250 and $600**. A compressor or a full-system quote is several thousand. Those are shop-level ranges, not a national survey. Use your own average ticket.

- A **$395/month** 100-minute live plan has to save you one ordinary no-cool job a month to be free, and that is before a long week blows the minutes.
- A **$300/month** 30-call live plan covers a quiet month and then bills **$11.50 a call** the moment a heat wave starts. Forty extra calls is another **$460**.
- A **$80–$150/month** flat or low-meter AI plan has to save you one ordinary no-cool job every two or three months.
- Missing **one** Saturday no-cool because nobody picked up wipes out any of those invoices.

## After-hours is the product. Daytime is a bonus.

A lot of searches are for an after-hours answering service that understands HVAC. The daytime coverage is easy. The 9pm no-cool is the thing you are buying.

Ask the provider to walk a real call, out loud:

The caller says the air stopped at 6pm. Indoor temp is 86. There is a two-year-old in the house. The address is 14 minutes from your shop. No burning smell. The unit is 11 years old and out of warranty.

You want to hear the questions they ask, the threshold at which they treat it as a same-night truck, and the exact way your on-call tech is reached: text, call, both, and what happens if you do not answer the first ping. If that walkthrough is vague in the sales call, it will be vague at 9pm too.

Then ask the staffing question a second time, with a date attached. "Who answers at 9pm on Sunday, 2 August, when the heat index is 108, and is that the same team I get at 10am on a Tuesday in March?" For a lot of live services the honest answer is no. After-hours is a different room, a different script, sometimes a different country. That is fine if you know it. It is a problem if you found out from a customer.

Heat-wave overflow is the other tell. A live centre that is excellent on a normal Thursday can queue your callers when half the city loses cooling on the same afternoon. Ask what happens when their queue spikes. An AI does not queue. A small live team does.

Winter has the mirror image of this problem. A no-heat call with a forecast in the 20s is the same economic event as a no-cool call at 98 degrees. The script should treat both as truck-out-now unless you have written a different rule.

## What to ask before you sign

Seven questions, in the order they will save you money:

1. **"Who answers at 9pm on a Sunday in a heat wave, and are they the same team as at 10am Tuesday?"** For a lot of providers the honest answer is no. That is the call you are buying.
2. **"Walk me through a no-cool, a no-heat, and a gas-smell call."** You want specific questions, a specific threshold, and a specific way your on-call tech is reached. If it is vague, it will be vague at 9pm too.
3. **"How do you bill a wrong number, a robocall, or a hang-up?"** On per-minute and per-call plans this is real money, and HVAC shops get a lot of parts-house and warranty noise.
4. **"What happens when your volume spikes on the same day mine does?"** A heat wave hits every HVAC company in the city at once. If the answering service queues, your caller dials the next shop.
5. **"Can the person answering tell a dead system from a dirty filter?"** No air at 86 degrees with a toddler in the house is a truck tonight. A homeowner who wants to know when you install mini-splits is Thursday. Somebody has to know the difference, and it is not the homeowner.
6. **"What do I actually receive after the call, and where does it land?"** A message you have to re-key is a message you will lose. Ask for a sample text or email from a real test call.
7. **"What's the cancellation term?"** Annual lock-ins are common in this category. Month-to-month is worth asking for, especially the first summer you run the service.

Do not skip the Sunday-night question. An HVAC answering service that is excellent at 10am and outsourced at 9pm is two different products sold as one.

## The four questions your answering service should ask every caller

Whatever you buy, the script matters more than the brand. For an HVAC company, four answers decide the night:

:::steps
Is the system blowing any air at all? :: Separates a same-night truck from a next-morning booking. "Warm air" and "no air" are different jobs. Get the caller to say which.
What's the indoor temperature, and is anyone vulnerable in the house? :: An 86-degree house with an infant, an elderly parent or a medical device is a different call from a 78-degree house with a slightly warm upstairs.
What's the address, and is it in our service area? :: Travel distance decides whether this job is worth taking tonight. So does the city limit you already refuse to cross.
Any burning smell, sparking, or a carbon monoxide alarm? :: That is no longer a comfort call. It is a safety call, and the script should say so and escalate immediately.
:::

Two more are worth adding if your shop cares about them: the age of the system, and whether it is still under a manufacturer or labour warranty. Those change the quote you can give before you leave the driveway.

An answering service that collects a name and a number has handed you a message. One that collects those four answers has handed you a qualified job you can price before you get in the van.
